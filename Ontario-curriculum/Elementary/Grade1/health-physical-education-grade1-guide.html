STRAND A · LESSON 1

Who Am I? Self-Awareness

Every person is unique. Learning to notice your own strengths, feelings, and needs is the first step to growing into the best version of yourself.

A1.1 Self-Awareness A1.2 Self-Monitoring

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

If someone asked you to describe yourself — but you couldn't mention what you look like — what would you say?

Think about this carefully. You might say you're funny, or kind, or that you love dinosaurs. But how do you actually know those things about yourself? Did someone tell you? Did you figure it out on your own? And here's the tricky part: could your best friend describe you the same way, or would they notice something completely different?

Self-awareness is the ability to see yourself clearly — your strengths, your struggles, and everything that makes you you. Today we begin the most important journey of all: learning about yourself.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • A1.1 I can identify my own strengths, interests, and things I am still learning to do.
  • A1.1 I can notice how my body feels when I am happy, sad, nervous, or excited.
  • A1.2 I can watch and notice my own behaviour and feelings during activities.
  • A1.2 I can describe one thing I am good at and one thing I want to improve.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

How to Introduce This Lesson

Begin by sharing something about yourself — a strength you're proud of and something you found hard to learn. ("When I was young, I found it hard to ride a bike. But I kept trying and eventually I learned!") This models the openness you want your child to develop.

Key Teaching Moments

  • Strengths vs. Challenges: Help your child understand that a "challenge" is not a weakness — it's simply a strength still being built. Avoid saying things like "you're not good at that." Say instead: "You're still learning that one — and that's exciting."
  • Body signals: Ask your child to notice what their body does when they feel nervous (butterflies in stomach, heart beating fast) versus happy (smiling, energy). These physical signals are incredibly valuable self-knowledge.
  • Uniqueness without comparison: Actively avoid comparing your child to siblings or peers during this lesson. Emphasize that each person is different, and different is good.

Questions to Ask

  • "What's something you did today that you're proud of?"
  • "Is there something you found hard lately? How did that feel in your body?"
  • "What do you think your superpower is — the thing you do really well?"

Signs of Real Understanding

Your child truly understands this lesson when they can articulate a specific strength (not just "I'm nice" but "I'm good at helping people when they're sad") and can identify at least one physical body signal connected to a feeling — without being prompted.

Common Misconception: Children often think "strength" means being good at sports or school subjects. Gently expand this: creativity, empathy, humor, persistence, and caring are also tremendous strengths.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Is Self-Awareness?

Imagine you have a special mirror — not one that shows your face, but one that shows your inside self. Self-awareness means you know yourself well. You notice what you're good at, what's hard for you, how you feel, and how you act around other people.

Scientists who study the brain call self-awareness one of the most important skills a human being can develop. Not all animals have it! Researchers have done experiments where they put a mirror in front of animals. Dolphins, great apes, elephants — and very few others — recognize that the reflection is themselves. Most animals don't. But you do! That self-recognition is the beginning of self-awareness.

🧠 Did You Know? Babies can't recognize themselves in a mirror — they learn this skill around 18–24 months old. A researcher named Gordon Gallup Jr. tested this in the 1970s by putting a dot on animals' faces and seeing if they would touch the dot when looking in a mirror. Only a few species did! Humans are one of them.

Your Strengths

A strength is something you do well. You might be a great listener. You might be really good at building things. Maybe you never give up when something is difficult. Maybe you make people laugh, or you're gentle with animals, or you ask excellent questions.

Here are examples of strengths that have NOTHING to do with sports or school marks:

  • Creativity: You come up with ideas that no one else thought of
  • Empathy: You notice when someone is sad and you want to help
  • Curiosity: You love asking "why" and "how" about everything
  • Persistence: You keep going even when things get hard
  • Humour: You know how to make people feel better by making them laugh
  • Fairness: You always want everyone to be treated the same

Your Challenges

A challenge is something you're still working on. Everyone — every single person on Earth, including the most successful adults — has things they find difficult. The trick is to notice your challenges without being unkind to yourself about them.

There's an important sentence that scientists and counselors use: "I can't do this YET." That one tiny word — yet — changes everything. It means your challenge is not permanent. Your brain is still growing and learning.

🔬 Did You Know? Your brain is not finished growing. It keeps building new connections every time you practice something, try something new, or push through something difficult. This is called neuroplasticity — and it means you can literally grow new abilities. Learning is physical!

Your Body Knows How You Feel

Here is something fascinating: your body sends you messages about your feelings before your brain even has words for them. Self-monitoring means noticing these body signals.

  • When you're nervous: butterflies in your stomach, heart beating faster, maybe sweaty palms
  • When you're excited: energy buzzing through your body, you might talk faster, your eyes go wide
  • When you're angry: heat in your face, your jaw might tighten, you feel like exploding
  • When you're happy: a lightness in your chest, a smile that just appears, energy flowing
  • When you're sad: heaviness, slowness, might want to be quiet or cry

A Canadian doctor named Dr. Daniel Siegel taught a simple idea: when you can name your feeling, it actually gets smaller and easier to manage. He called it "name it to tame it." So the next time you feel something big, try to name it — and notice where you feel it in your body.

🔬 Real Scientist: Dr. Daniel Siegel is a professor at UCLA who studies how the brain and feelings connect. He invented a technique called the "Hand Model of the Brain" to teach kids how feelings work. He's also the author of many books for parents about how to raise emotionally healthy children.

STRAND A · LESSON 2

My Feelings and Emotions

Feelings are information, not problems. Learning to name, understand, and express your emotions is one of the most powerful skills you will ever develop.

A1.1 Self-Awareness A1.5 Communication

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

Why do humans have feelings at all? If feelings make life so complicated, why didn't our brains just evolve WITHOUT them?

Think about it: feelings can be uncomfortable. When you're scared, your heart pounds. When you're sad, everything feels heavy. When you're angry, you might say something you didn't mean. Wouldn't life be easier without all of that?

Or would it? Before this lesson is over, you're going to discover that your feelings are not your enemies — they are your oldest, most loyal advisors. They've been protecting humans for millions of years. And the better you understand them, the more powerful you become.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • A1.1 I can name a wide range of feelings and describe what causes them.
  • A1.1 I can explain that all feelings are okay, but not all behaviours are okay.
  • A1.5 I can use words to communicate how I feel instead of acting out.
  • A1.5 I can use "I feel" statements to express my emotions to others.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

How to Introduce This Lesson

Before teaching this lesson, do a brief emotion check-in with yourself. Try saying aloud: "Right now I feel _______ because _______." Model the process you want your child to learn. Children learn emotional vocabulary primarily from hearing it used naturally by adults.

Key Teaching Moments

  • Validate before you redirect: The single most important thing you can do is say "It makes sense that you feel that way" before attempting to solve the problem or correct behavior. This is neurologically important — a child whose feelings are validated can regulate much more quickly.
  • Feelings ≠ Actions: Clearly distinguish between the feeling (always okay) and the action (might not be okay). "It makes sense that you're angry. But throwing things is not okay — let's find another way to show your anger."
  • "I feel" statements: Teach the formula: "I feel [emotion] when [situation] because [reason]." Practice this with your child during this lesson.

What to Say If Your Child Struggles

Some children find it very hard to name feelings. If your child says "I don't know" how they feel, try asking about their body instead: "What does your tummy feel like right now?" or "Are your shoulders relaxed or tight?" The body is often more accessible than abstract emotion words.

Success Indicator

Your child genuinely understands this lesson when, in a moment of real emotion (not during the lesson), they say something like "I feel frustrated because..." instead of melting down or acting out. This may take weeks of practice after the lesson — that's perfectly normal.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Why Do We Have Feelings?

Hundreds of thousands of years ago, your ancestors lived in a very dangerous world — wild animals, enemy tribes, unpredictable weather, and food that wasn't always safe. They needed a fast warning system. They needed their brains to instantly recognize danger and protect them. That warning system is your emotions.

  • Fear made them run from predators — and kept them alive
  • Anger gave them the energy to fight when needed
  • Disgust stopped them from eating rotten food
  • Sadness helped them process loss and rebuild
  • Joy told them what was worth repeating
  • Surprise snapped them to attention when something unexpected happened

These "basic emotions" were identified by a scientist named Dr. Paul Ekman. He discovered something incredible: people in countries around the world — even countries that had never had contact with each other — made the same facial expressions for these basic emotions. That means emotions are universal — all humans share them, regardless of language or culture.

😲 Did You Know? In the early 1960s, Dr. Paul Ekman traveled to Papua New Guinea to meet a tribe that had never seen movies or television and had almost no contact with the outside world. He showed them photos of facial expressions and they correctly identified the emotions. Emotions are written in our biology — they are part of what it means to be human.

The Emotion Spectrum

There are far more than 6 emotions. Scientists now think humans experience 27 distinct categories of emotion. Here are some you might experience in a typical day:

  • Happy · Excited · Proud · Grateful · Calm · Playful · Loved
  • Sad · Disappointed · Lonely · Worried · Nervous · Embarrassed
  • Angry · Frustrated · Jealous · Annoyed · Irritated
  • Confused · Surprised · Curious · Bored · Overwhelmed

Notice how many more words there are than just "good" and "bad"! The more emotion words you know, the better you can understand and explain your inner world. Researchers at the University of California found that people who could use specific emotion words (like "frustrated" rather than just "bad") were much better at managing their feelings.

Important: All Feelings Are Okay

Here is one of the most important things you will ever learn: every feeling is okay to have. You cannot choose what you feel — feelings just arrive, like weather. You can't stop rain from falling, but you can choose whether to stand in it or go inside.

What this means is: your feelings are never wrong. But your actions can be. The goal is to feel the feeling, understand why it's there, and choose a helpful response.

🎬 Fun Connection: The Pixar movie Inside Out was made with help from actual scientists, including Dr. Ekman and Dr. Dacher Keltner from Berkeley University. The creators wanted to get the science of emotions right! They studied how emotions live inside our brains and how they work together.

Communicating Your Feelings

The most powerful tool for handling emotions is simple: use your words. Instead of yelling, crying, shutting down, or hitting — try this formula:

"I feel [emotion] when [situation] because [reason]."

Example: "I feel frustrated when we have to stop playing because I was really enjoying it and I wasn't ready to stop." Notice how this is completely different from "THAT'S NOT FAIR!" Both express the same feeling, but one helps people understand you.

Solving Problems and Thinking Creatively

Problems are not obstacles — they are invitations to think. Learning to solve problems creatively is a superpower that will help you for your entire life.

A1.2 Adaptive Thinking A1.2 Critical Thinking A1.3 Motivation

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

If you had no scissors, no tape, and no ruler — but you needed to make a perfectly square piece of paper from a rectangular sheet — could you figure out how?

Stop and actually think about this before reading on. (You fold the corner until it lines up perfectly, then tear along the crease.) The point isn't the answer — it's that you FOUND the answer without tools. Your brain solved it using nothing but creative thinking.

Problems come in all shapes and sizes. Some have one right answer. Some have a thousand possible solutions. The most important skill isn't memorizing answers — it's knowing HOW to think when you're stuck. That's what today is about.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • A1.2 I can describe what "problem solving" means and why it's important in daily life and physical activity.
  • A1.2 I can use a simple problem-solving process: Stop → Think → Choose → Try → Reflect.
  • A1.2 I can come up with more than one solution to a problem and choose the best one.
  • A1.3 I can keep trying even when my first solution doesn't work.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

The Most Important Shift

The biggest gift you can give a young problem-solver is resisting the urge to solve things for them. When your child is stuck, try waiting 30 extra seconds before helping. Ask guiding questions rather than giving answers: "What have you tried so far?" "What else could you try?" "What would happen if...?"

Key Concepts to Reinforce

  • Adaptive thinking: Changing your approach when one thing isn't working
  • Critical thinking: Asking "is this really the best solution?" before jumping in
  • Divergent thinking: Coming up with MANY possible solutions before picking one

Common Misconception to Address

Many children believe there is ONE right answer to every problem. In real life — especially in physical activity, relationships, and creative work — there are usually multiple valid solutions. Actively celebrate when your child finds an unusual or unexpected solution.

You Can Say:

"I love that you tried a different way when the first way didn't work. That's exactly what great thinkers do."

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Is a Problem?

A problem is any situation where you want something to be different. You want to make a goal but you can't figure out how to get past the defender. You want to make friends but you don't know what to say. You want to build a tower but it keeps falling over. These are all problems — and they all have solutions.

Here's the interesting thing: problems are actually how learning happens. Every time you successfully solve a problem, your brain builds new connections. Problems are your brain's gym equipment.

The 5-Step Problem Solver

Scientists and engineers use versions of this process for everything from landing on the moon to inventing new medicines:

  1. STOP: Pause. Take a breath. Don't react immediately. Just notice: "There is a problem here."
  2. THINK: Ask yourself: What exactly is the problem? What do I want to happen? What have I already tried?
  3. CHOOSE: Come up with at least 2-3 possible solutions. Think about what might happen with each one. Choose the most promising.
  4. TRY: Go ahead and try your chosen solution. Be brave — even if you're not sure it will work.
  5. REFLECT: Afterwards, ask: Did it work? What would I do differently? What did I learn?
🚀 Did You Know? The engineers who sent humans to the moon in 1969 faced thousands of problems they had never encountered before. On the Apollo 13 mission, an oxygen tank exploded in space. The crew had to use creative thinking — using materials from around the spacecraft — to build a new air filter just to breathe. They solved it in hours. That's the power of systematic problem-solving under pressure.

Thinking Creatively vs. Thinking Critically

Creative thinking means making lots of ideas — even silly ones, even impossible ones. The rule is: no idea is a bad idea in the creative phase. Quantity first, quality second.

Critical thinking means looking carefully at your ideas and asking: Is this realistic? Does this help everyone or just me? What are the downsides? This is the "sorting" phase.

The best problem-solvers switch between these two modes. First they dream big (creative), then they get practical (critical).

🌍 In Physical Activity: Problem-solving in games and sports happens constantly. Your team is losing by 2 points with 3 minutes left. What do you change? How do you adapt? Who takes the important position? Great athletes are great problem-solvers — not just physically skilled people.

When Problems Feel Too Big

Some problems feel overwhelming — too big to even know where to start. Here's a secret: break it into the smallest possible pieces and solve just one piece at a time. This is called chunking. Even the largest bridge in the world was built one section at a time.

STRAND A · LESSON 4

Keep Trying! Goals and Perseverance

Almost every great achievement in history happened because someone refused to quit when things got hard. Perseverance is not stubbornness — it is the patient, creative commitment to what matters.

A1.3 Positive Motivation A1.4 Goal Setting

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

Thomas Edison tried over 1,000 different materials before finding one that worked in a lightbulb. How did he NOT give up? What was going on in his brain?

When a reporter asked Edison about his 1,000 "failures," he reportedly said: "I have not failed. I've just found 1,000 ways that won't work." That shift in thinking — from "I failed" to "I found information" — is called a growth mindset. And it is the engine behind almost every great human achievement. Today you'll discover what it takes to keep going when everything in you wants to stop.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • A1.3 I can explain what perseverance means and why it matters in physical activity and in life.
  • A1.3 I can identify strategies to help me keep trying when I feel like giving up.
  • A1.4 I can set a SMART goal (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound).
  • A1.4 I can describe the steps I need to take to reach a goal and track my progress.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

The research on motivation and perseverance (especially Dr. Carol Dweck's work on growth mindset at Stanford University) consistently shows that children who are praised for effort and process — rather than talent or results — develop much stronger perseverance than those who are told they are "smart" or "naturally good."

What to Say Instead

  • Instead of "You're so smart!" → "You worked so hard on that — what strategy did you use?"
  • Instead of "You're naturally good at that" → "All that practice is really showing up!"
  • Instead of "You'll get it eventually" → "What's one small step you could try differently?"

Helping With Goal Setting

When your child sets a goal during this lesson, help them make it as specific as possible. "I want to get better at jumping" is not a goal — it's a wish. "I want to be able to jump over a skipping rope 5 times in a row by next week" is a goal. The specificity is what makes it actionable.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Is Perseverance?

Perseverance means you keep going. Not blindly, not stubbornly — but with purpose and creativity. It means when you hit a wall, you look for a door.

Dr. Angela Duckworth, a researcher at the University of Pennsylvania, studied what made people succeed in life. She studied West Point military cadets, spelling bee champions, and first-year teachers in tough neighbourhoods. What predicted success? Not IQ. Not talent. Not family wealth. The single biggest predictor was what she called GRIT — passion plus perseverance for long-term goals.

⚽ Did You Know? Before Lionel Messi became arguably the greatest soccer player who ever lived, doctors told his family at age 10 that he had a growth hormone deficiency and needed expensive medical treatment. His family couldn't afford it. His club, FC Barcelona, offered to pay for his treatment if he moved from Argentina to Spain. He was scared. He was far from home. He was still growing. But he persevered — and the rest is history.

Growth Mindset vs. Fixed Mindset

Dr. Carol Dweck at Stanford University discovered something that changed how teachers and coaches think about learning. She found that children have one of two beliefs about their abilities:

  • Fixed mindset: "I'm either good at this or I'm not. Ability is something you're born with." (These children avoid challenges and give up quickly.)
  • Growth mindset: "My abilities can grow with effort and practice. I'm not good at this YET." (These children seek challenges and persist longer.)

Here's the good news: mindsets can change. Just learning about the growth mindset — right now, as you read this — actually shifts how your brain thinks about challenges. Your brain is literally restructuring as you absorb this idea.

How to Set a SMART Goal

Wishing is different from planning. A SMART goal turns a wish into a plan:

  • S — Specific: Exactly what do you want to achieve? ("Skip rope 10 times in a row" not "get better at skipping")
  • M — Measurable: How will you know when you've achieved it? Can you count it? Time it?
  • A — Achievable: Is this realistic? Can you actually get there with effort?
  • R — Relevant: Does this matter to YOU? Goals you choose are more powerful than goals others set for you.
  • T — Time-bound: When will you reach this goal? Give yourself a date.
🏃 In Physical Activity: Setting fitness goals is a perfect SMART goal opportunity. Instead of "I want to run faster," try: "I want to run around the block without stopping by the end of the month. I will practice 3 times per week."

Strategies When You Want to Give Up

The Three Communication Styles — Know Them

  • Passive: "Whatever you want" — suppressing your needs to avoid conflict. Leads to resentment.
  • Aggressive: "My way or no way" — prioritizing your needs while ignoring others'. Damages relationships.
  • Assertive: "Here's what I need, and I'd like to hear what you need too." This is the goal.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

How Communication Actually Works

Researchers have studied how humans communicate and found something surprising: what you actually SAY (the words themselves) is a surprisingly small part of how your message is received. Albert Mehrabian's famous study found that:

  • Words account for about 7% of emotional communication
  • Tone of voice (how you say it) accounts for 38%
  • Body language (face, posture, gestures) accounts for 55%

This explains why the same sentence — "Fine, do whatever you want" — can mean completely different things depending on how you say it and what your face is doing.

🗣️ Did You Know? The human face can make over 10,000 distinct expressions. We are extraordinarily good at reading these — even babies can tell the difference between a happy face and a sad face before they understand any words. Your face is constantly communicating, even when you're silent.

Active Listening — The Rarest Skill

Most people listen just enough to know when it's their turn to talk. Active listening is completely different. It means:

  • Stopping what you're doing and giving full attention
  • Making eye contact (in cultures where this is comfortable)
  • NOT thinking about what you'll say next while they're still talking
  • Nodding or giving small sounds to show you're following
  • Asking questions when you're not sure what they meant
  • Repeating back what you heard: "So what you're saying is..."

Active listening is one of the most valued skills in the world. Therapists spend years learning to do it. Doctors who actively listen have patients who recover faster. Leaders who actively listen have teams that perform better.

Three Styles of Communication

There are three main ways people express their needs and respond to others:

  • Passive: You hide what you think or feel. You go along with things even when they bother you. ("It's fine, whatever.") This seems kind but often builds up frustration and doesn't solve problems.
  • Aggressive: You express yourself forcefully without caring about the other person's feelings. ("I don't care what you think — we're doing it MY way!") This creates conflict and damages friendships.
  • Assertive: You express yourself clearly and honestly while still respecting the other person. ("I would really like to try it this way. What do you think?") This is the goal.
🏀 In Sports and Games: Teams that communicate assertively during a game perform better. Instead of yelling at a teammate who made an error (aggressive) or saying nothing and feeling frustrated (passive), an assertive player might say: "Next time you're in that position, pass it to me — I'll be open on your left."

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Makes a Good Friend?

Think about your best friend, or the person you like spending time with most. What makes them someone you want to be around? Research on friendship has identified the qualities that matter most — and interestingly, they're not about being popular or funny or cool. They're about something much more fundamental:

  • Trustworthiness: They keep your secrets and follow through on promises
  • Loyalty: They stick by you even when things are hard
  • Empathy: They care about how you feel
  • Honesty: They tell you the truth, even when it's not easy to hear
  • Inclusion: They make sure no one is left out
  • Reciprocity: They give as much as they receive — friendship is balanced
🧬 Did You Know? A study of chimpanzees found that they form genuine long-term friendships with specific individuals — they groom the same friends repeatedly, stay close to them during stressful situations, and help them out. This means friendship may be millions of years old, and it was already important long before humans existed!

How to Make New Friends

Making new friends can feel scary. But research shows that the most effective way to start a friendship is surprisingly simple: consistent proximity + repeated interactions. In other words — be near the same people regularly, and talk to them.

Some practical strategies:

  • Ask questions: People love to talk about themselves. "What's your favourite game?" works better than "Hi, I'm ___."
  • Find common ground: "Oh, you like that too? Me too!" — shared interests are the fastest friendship bridge
  • Be a joiner: Say yes to invitations even when you're nervous
  • Be consistent: Show up. Friendships are built over time through repeated contact

Handling Disagreements Like a Pro

Every friendship has conflicts — even the best ones. In fact, a friendship that never has any disagreements might mean someone is always giving in. Conflict is normal. How you handle it is what matters.

The steps for healthy conflict with a friend:

  1. Cool down first: Don't say anything you'll regret. Take space if you need it.
  2. See their side: Before explaining yourself, genuinely try to understand their perspective
  3. Use "I feel" not "You always": "I feel hurt when..." vs. "You always make me feel..."
  4. Listen without defending: When they talk, just listen — don't plan your response yet
  5. Find a solution together: What can BOTH of you do differently?
  6. Forgive: Genuine forgiveness doesn't mean forgetting — it means choosing not to carry anger

What About Being Left Out?

Everyone experiences being left out at some point. It hurts — physically. Brain scans show that social rejection activates the same areas as physical pain. But there are things you can do:

  • If you're being left out: Talk about it. Find different people to play with. Remember: being left out once doesn't define your worth.
  • If you see someone else being left out: This is your moment. Walk over. Include them. One person's courage to include changes everything.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Your Body Was Built to Move

Humans evolved over millions of years as active creatures. Our ancestors walked an average of 10–15 kilometres every single day — hunting, gathering, building, playing, exploring. Our bones, muscles, hearts, lungs, and especially our brains evolved to work best when we are regularly moving.

Today, children and adults often sit for much of the day. But our bodies haven't changed — they still expect movement. When we don't get enough, our bodies and brains start to suffer. Physical inactivity is actually one of the leading preventable health problems in the world.

🧠 Did You Know? Dr. John Ratey, a professor at Harvard Medical School, calls exercise "miracle-gro for the brain." Every time you exercise, your brain releases a protein called BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor). BDNF actually builds new brain cells and connections — the same structures your brain uses to learn and remember. Exercise is literally building your brain.

What Happens to Your Body During Exercise

When you start to move vigorously, a fascinating chain of events happens:

  • Your muscles need more oxygen, so they signal your heart and lungs to work harder
  • Your heart rate increases — it pumps faster to deliver oxygen-rich blood to your muscles
  • Your breathing rate increases — you breathe faster to bring in more oxygen
  • Your blood vessels widen to allow more blood flow to your muscles
  • Your temperature rises, and you start to sweat to cool down
  • Your brain releases endorphins — chemicals that make you feel good, reduce pain, and improve mood
  • Your brain also releases dopamine — a chemical connected to motivation, reward, and focus

This is why you often feel BETTER after exercise than before — even when you didn't feel like moving at first!

The Many Benefits of Regular Physical Activity

  • Stronger body: Muscles, bones, and heart all get stronger with regular use
  • Better focus: Research consistently shows that active children pay attention better in learning
  • Better mood: Exercise is one of the most reliable mood-boosters known to science
  • Better sleep: People who are physically active fall asleep faster and sleep more deeply
  • More energy: Paradoxically, using energy through exercise creates more energy
  • Social connection: Many physical activities are social — they bring people together
  • Creative thinking: Walking and other rhythmic movement boosts creative thinking (this is why so many writers, inventors, and thinkers throughout history were famous walkers)
🔬 Real Researcher: Dr. Wendy Suzuki is a neuroscientist at New York University who studies the effect of exercise on the brain. She was so amazed by the changes in her brain and mood that she changed her entire research focus to exercise neuroscience. Her TED Talk has been watched millions of times.

The research is clear: the best kind of exercise is the kind you actually enjoy, because you'll do it consistently.

Think about: What makes your body want to move? What activity makes you forget to look at the clock? That's your personal answer — and there's no wrong answer.

  • Running: Arms bent at 90°, pumping forward and back (not crossing the body), landing on middle of foot, leaning slightly forward
  • Jumping: Bend knees before takeoff, swing arms up, land softly on both feet with bent knees ("quiet landing")
  • Hopping: Same foot takes off and lands; balance is key; arms help with lift
  • Skipping: Step-hop on one foot, then step-hop on the other; alternating rhythm; takes most children longer to learn
  • Galloping: One foot leads, other foot follows and catches up; faster than a walk, less even than a skip

What to Watch For

"Quiet landings" are important — children who stomp when they land are at higher risk of knee and ankle injury. Praise soft, bent-knee landings every time you notice them.

What Are Locomotor Skills?

Locomotor skills are movements that take your body from one place to another. They are the foundation of almost all sports and physical activities. When children develop these skills well, they are more likely to enjoy and continue being physically active throughout their lives.

The Eight Core Locomotor Skills

1. WALKING: The most basic locomotor skill. One foot is always on the ground. Arms swing naturally in opposition (right arm forward when left foot steps). Good posture: head up, shoulders relaxed, eyes forward, not down.

2. RUNNING: Similar to walking but faster, with a flight phase — a moment when BOTH feet are off the ground. Arms should be bent at 90° and pump straight forward and back (not crossing your body — that wastes energy). Land on the middle of your foot, not your heel. The faster you run, the more you lean forward from the ankles.

3. JUMPING: Taking off from one or both feet and landing on both feet. Before you jump, bend your knees and swing your arms back. As you push off, drive your arms upward to create lift. Land with bent knees to absorb the impact — this protects your joints and makes you quieter.

4. HOPPING: Jumping off one foot and landing on the same foot. This requires more balance than jumping. Try hopping 5 times on your right foot, then 5 times on your left. Which side is stronger? Most people are surprised to discover they have a dominant side.

5. SKIPPING: A step-hop pattern that alternates feet: step on the right, hop on the right, step on the left, hop on the left. It has a musical, rhythmic quality. Interestingly, skipping is one of the later locomotor skills to develop — many 5-year-olds are still learning it, and that's perfectly normal. It requires coordination between both sides of the brain.

6. GALLOPING: One foot leads, the other follows and catches up. The leading foot always stays in front. Galloping is often one of the first "combined" patterns children learn — it feels fast and fun!

7. SLIDING: Moving sideways with a step-together-step pattern. Your feet stay close to the ground. This skill is fundamental for sports like basketball, tennis, and soccer where you need to move sideways quickly.

8. LEAPING: Like a long running step, but bigger — you take off from one foot, fly through the air, and land on the opposite foot. Ballet dancers leap. Long jumpers leap. A well-executed leap feels like flying.

🐣 Did You Know? Babies learn to walk by falling and getting up hundreds of times. Researchers at New York University found that toddlers learning to walk fall an average of 17 times per HOUR and take around 2,368 steps per day. Learning to walk is one of the hardest things a human ever does — and you did it before you even turned 2!

Combining Locomotor Skills

Real life rarely uses just one locomotor skill at a time. You run, then leap over a puddle. You gallop toward the ball, then slide sideways to reach it. You hop on one foot over an obstacle, then run. Combining locomotor skills fluidly is a sign of excellent movement coordination — and it's just plain fun to practice.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

The Science of Balance

Your ability to balance comes from a remarkable team of three systems working together:

  • Your vestibular system: The fluid-filled canals in your inner ear that detect movement and position of your head
  • Your visual system: Your eyes that tell your brain where you are in space relative to things around you
  • Your proprioceptive system: Thousands of sensors in your muscles, joints, and skin that tell your brain exactly where each body part is positioned

Your brain receives all this information and makes tiny, constant adjustments — contracting and releasing muscles dozens of times per second — to keep you upright. This happens automatically, without you thinking about it. It's one of the most complex things your brain does.

🤸 Did You Know? Professional ballet dancers are specifically trained to resist dizziness. They do this by "spotting" — fixing their eyes on one point as they spin, then whipping their head around to that same point with each rotation. This trains their brain to suppress the dizziness signals from the inner ear. It's a trainable skill that takes years to develop.

Centre of Gravity

Every object has a centre of gravity — the point where all its weight seems to be balanced. For humans, it's roughly in the middle of your belly, just below your belly button. You are stable when your centre of gravity is directly above your base of support (the surface your feet touch).

This is why:

  • Standing on two feet is easier than one (bigger base of support)
  • Crouching low is more stable than standing tall (lower centre of gravity)
  • Holding your arms out helps when balancing (it shifts your weight to keep your centre of gravity centred)
  • Sumo wrestlers spread their feet wide and crouch low (maximum stability)

Types of Balance Activities

Static balance means balancing without moving — like a statue. Examples:

  • Standing on one foot (eyes open, then closed)
  • The "tree pose" from yoga — standing on one foot, arms up
  • Balancing on your hands and knees
  • Balancing while standing on your tiptoes

Dynamic balance means balancing while moving. Examples:

  • Walking on a line on the floor (one foot directly in front of the other)
  • Walking on a balance beam or log
  • Catching an object while standing on one foot
  • Galloping then stopping quickly and balancing

Body Rolling

Rolling your body in a controlled way is a fundamental gymnastics skill. The most important things about safe rolling:

  • Forward roll: Tuck your chin to your chest. Bend your knees. Place hands flat on the mat, shoulder-width apart. Push off and roll on the rounded part of your back — not your head or neck.
  • Sideways roll (log roll): Lie flat with arms above your head. Keep your body long and straight. Roll using your whole body together.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Are Object Control Skills?

Object control skills are movements that involve interacting with an object. They include: throwing, catching, kicking, striking (with a bat, paddle, or hand), bouncing, rolling, and trapping. These skills are fundamental to almost every team sport.

The Overhand Throw

The overhand throw is one of the most distinctive human skills. Chimpanzees — our closest relatives — can throw, but they are far less accurate than humans. Researchers believe that precise throwing was such an important survival skill for early humans (for hunting) that our shoulder joints actually evolved specifically to allow powerful, accurate throwing.

Key mechanics of a good overhand throw:

  1. Grip: Hold the ball in your fingers, not your palm (better control)
  2. Stance: Turn sideways with your non-throwing shoulder pointing at the target
  3. Wind-up: Draw the ball back past your ear (the further back, the more power)
  4. Step: Step forward with the foot OPPOSITE your throwing arm — this is crucial for power
  5. Release: Snap your wrist as you release the ball (spin creates accuracy)
  6. Follow-through: Your throwing arm continues across your body — stopping it early kills your power
⚾ Did You Know? The fastest accurately recorded human throw was by Aroldis Chapman in 2010 — he threw a baseball at 105.1 miles per hour (169 km/h). His arm movement during that throw lasted about 0.1 seconds. That's faster than a blink of an eye!

Catching: The Art of "Soft Hands"

Bad catching happens when people:

  • Watch the thrower instead of the ball
  • Turn their head away at the last second
  • Keep their arms stiff instead of reaching toward the ball
  • Close their hands too early or too late

Good catching requires tracking — following the ball with your eyes all the way into your hands. This is harder than it sounds. The brain must calculate where the ball will arrive and move your hands there in advance. "Soft hands" means you let your hands give slightly as the ball arrives — absorbing its momentum instead of fighting it.

Kicking

When kicking a ball for accuracy, the most important technique is the non-kicking foot. Where you plant it determines where the ball goes. Plant it beside the ball, pointing at your target. Strike with the flat inner part of your foot (not your toe — that's for power, not accuracy). Follow through toward the target.

Striking

Striking (hitting an object with a paddle, bat, or hand) requires timing above all. The swing needs to connect with the object exactly at the right moment. Starting with a large, slow-moving object (balloon) and gradually making it smaller and faster is the most effective progression.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

The Language of Movement

Physical educators and dance teachers use a framework called movement concepts to describe HOW a body moves. Understanding these concepts turns movement from something you do automatically into something you can understand, analyze, and choose deliberately.

SPACE: Where You Move

  • Self-space: The space directly around your body that only you occupy — like a bubble around you. You can reach in all directions without touching anyone else.
  • General space: The entire area available for everyone to move through together. Moving in general space means being aware of all the other people and objects around you.
  • Pathways: The "trail" your movement makes through space — straight, curved, or zigzag.

DIRECTION: Which Way You Move

  • Forward — the most common, easiest direction
  • Backward — requires more awareness and slower speed for safety
  • Sideways — sliding, shuffling left or right
  • Diagonal — cutting corners, angling across a space
  • Clockwise / Counterclockwise — for turning movements

LEVEL: How High Your Body Is

  • High level: Stretching up, jumping, reaching — your body at its tallest
  • Medium level: Normal upright movement
  • Low level: Crouching, crawling, rolling — your body close to the ground

Changing level changes your speed, your stability, your visibility in a game, and your mechanical advantage in different situations. An ice hockey goalie drops to low level to block; a basketball player extends to high level to rebound.

SPEED: How Fast You Move

  • Fast: Maximum velocity — all-out effort
  • Medium: Jogging, moderate pace — sustainable for longer
  • Slow: Deliberate, controlled — important for balance, precision, and reading a situation
  • Acceleration / Deceleration: Speeding up or slowing down — often more important than top speed
⚽ Did You Know? Studies of professional soccer players found that during a 90-minute match, elite players change direction over 700 times. They almost never run in a straight line for more than 10 seconds. Their movement intelligence — constantly reading space, changing direction, varying speed — is what makes them elite, not just their raw speed or fitness.

Key Concepts

  • Aerobic activity: Exercise that gets the heart and lungs working harder — sustained, rhythmic movement (running, swimming, cycling, dancing)

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Your Amazing Heart

Your heart is a hollow muscle roughly the size of your fist. It sits in the centre of your chest, slightly to the left. It has four chambers — two on top (atria) and two on the bottom (ventricles) — and it works like a double pump.

  • The right side of your heart receives blood from your body that has delivered its oxygen, and pumps it to your lungs to pick up more oxygen
  • The left side receives oxygen-rich blood from your lungs and pumps it out to the rest of your body
  • This happens simultaneously, 60–100 times per minute, every minute of every day — including while you sleep
❤️ Did You Know? If you stretched out all the blood vessels in your body end to end, they would extend about 100,000 kilometres — enough to circle the Earth 2.5 times! And your heart pumps blood through all of them, about 7,500 litres of blood per day. The entire journey of blood from heart to body and back takes about 20 seconds.

Your Lungs

Your lungs are your oxygen intake system. Inside each lung are millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli — so small you'd need a microscope to see them. They have incredibly thin walls, which allows oxygen to pass directly from your breath into your blood, and carbon dioxide to pass from your blood back into the air you exhale.

Your two lungs together have a surface area about the size of a tennis court — all folded into your chest cavity. That enormous surface area is what makes gas exchange so efficient.

What Happens During Exercise

When you start exercising, your muscles demand more oxygen immediately. Your body responds:

  1. Your brain signals the heart to beat faster
  2. Your heart pumps more blood with each beat AND beats more often
  3. Your lungs breathe faster and deeper to bring in more oxygen
  4. Blood vessels in your muscles widen, bringing more oxygen-rich blood there
  5. Your body temperature rises; you sweat to cool down

Long-Term Benefits for the Heart

Here's the extraordinary thing: the more you challenge your heart through aerobic exercise, the stronger and more efficient it becomes. A fit heart pumps more blood with each beat — so it doesn't need to beat as many times per minute to do the same job. This is why athletes often have resting heart rates of 40–50 beats per minute, while an average adult is 70–80. Their hearts are simply more powerful.

Cardiovascular endurance is the fitness of your heart and lungs for sustained activity. Building it through regular aerobic exercise means you can run, play, and move for longer without getting breathless — and your heart stays healthier throughout your life.

Playing Together: Fair Play and Inclusion

Physical activity is more than a personal pursuit. When we play together, we build community, practice fairness, and create the kind of world we want to live in — one game at a time.

Games have existed in every culture in human history — ancient Egyptians played board games, Greeks had the Olympics, and even animals play. Why do humans need to play? What would happen to a child who never played?

Research by Dr. Stuart Brown at the National Institute for Play found that play-deprived animals become aggressive, socially impaired, and cannot handle stress well. Play isn't frivolous — it is how brains develop empathy, negotiation, creativity, and resilience. And fair play in physical games teaches all these things simultaneously. 👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

Children learn sportsmanship primarily from watching adults. They closely observe how you respond when you lose a game, make a mistake, or witness unfairness. The language you use about athletes on TV ("the other team cheated!" or "that referee was terrible!") teaches them how to respond in their own games.

Key Teaching Points

  • Include everyone: Actively modify games so every child can participate meaningfully — not as a token inclusion but as a genuine contributor
  • Winning isn't the point: The goal of fair play in childhood is the experience and the relationship — not the score
  • Model losing well: Play games with your child where you genuinely try to win, and sometimes lose — then model grace in losing

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Is Fair Play?

Fair play is about much more than not cheating. It includes:

  • Following the rules: Even when no one is watching, even when breaking them would help you win
  • Respecting your opponents: They are not your enemies — they are the partners who make the game possible
  • Respecting the officials: Even when you disagree with a call
  • Winning with grace: Not bragging or embarrassing the other team
  • Losing with grace: Congratulating the winner genuinely, not sulking
  • Playing your best: Giving full effort is itself a form of respect to everyone involved
🏅 Did You Know? The Fair Play Award is given at the Olympics to athletes who demonstrate exceptional sportsmanship — sometimes even at the cost of their own medal. In 2016, two runners who collided and fell during a race helped each other up and finished together, even though finishing separately would have earned one of them a medal. They both received standing ovations — and were considered winners by everyone watching.

Including Everyone

Inclusion means making sure everyone can participate meaningfully — not just physically present, but truly involved. Some ways to make games more inclusive:

  • Modify the rules: Change the equipment, distance, or rules to make the game accessible (use a bigger ball, a shorter net, or extra tries)
  • Take turns being "it" or having the advantage: Rotate positions so everyone gets to experience different roles
  • Celebrate different contributions: Praise passing, helping, encouraging, not just scoring
  • Never leave anyone "last picked": Use random selection, or better yet — mix teams in advance

Winning, Losing, and Growing

Research shows that children who learn to lose well are more resilient, more emotionally regulated, and more enjoyable to play with — which means they get invited to play more often. Paradoxically, learning to lose well leads to more wins in the long run.

The most important question after any game isn't "Did we win?" but "Did we play well? Did we learn something? Was it worth doing?" These questions turn every game into a success regardless of the score.

Scientists found that children who are physically active for 60 minutes per day are not only healthier — they also do better in school, have more friends, feel better about themselves, and sleep more soundly. If being active has all these benefits, why do so many people struggle to stay active?

The answer, researchers have found, is habits. Habits are powerful, automatic routines that our brains form around repeated behaviours. The good news: they can be built deliberately. Today, you'll learn the science of habit formation — and you'll create your own personal active living plan that you can actually stick with.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

The Role of Environment

Research on habit formation shows clearly that the environment is more powerful than willpower. If you want your child to be active daily, make active choices the easy, obvious, default choices: keep balls and bikes easily accessible, build active time into the daily schedule rather than waiting for motivation, make physical activity a family activity rather than a solo chore.

  • Include at least 3 days of vigorous activity (e.g., running, jumping, active sports) per week

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

The Science of Habits

Researcher Charles Duhigg, author of The Power of Habit, found that habits work in a 3-step loop:

  1. Cue: A trigger that tells your brain to start the habitual behaviour (e.g., seeing your shoes by the door)
  2. Routine: The behaviour itself (e.g., putting on your shoes and going outside to play)
  3. Reward: The good feeling that reinforces the habit (e.g., the joy of running outside)

To build an activity habit, you need to design all three parts. The cue should be something predictable in your environment. The routine should be something you genuinely enjoy (otherwise you'll skip it). The reward should be something that actually feels good — and the natural reward of physical activity (endorphins, fresh air, fun) is powerful if you remember to notice it.

What Helps You Stay Active?

Researchers call these supports and barriers. Common supports:

  • Having equipment easily available (bike, ball, jump rope)
  • Having someone active to be with
  • Having a fun activity you genuinely love
  • Building activity into your daily routine (not treating it as optional)
  • Living or being near places to be active (parks, trails, yards)

Common barriers:

  • Screen time that competes for attention
  • Feeling tired, busy, or unmotivated
  • Believing you need special equipment or a "sport" to be active
  • Bad weather (real barrier — plan for it!)
  • Not having a partner to be active with

Canada's Physical Activity Guidelines

Canada's guidelines for children your age recommend at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity every day. This sounds like a lot, but it doesn't need to happen all at once. It can be broken into chunks — 15 minutes here, 20 minutes there. The key is making sure it happens every day.

🍁 Canadian Fact: According to the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, only about 39% of Canadian children ages 5-11 meet the recommended 60 minutes of physical activity per day. That means over half of Canadian children are not getting enough movement to support their developing brains and bodies. You can be part of changing that statistic — starting with yourself.

Keeping Safe at Home & School

Safety rules are not meant to limit your fun — they are there to protect you so you can have fun for a very long time. Learning to recognize safe and unsafe situations is one of the most important skills you can develop.

C1.1 Personal Safety

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

If you found scissors on the floor near a sleeping baby, what would you do — and how did you know what to do?

Something inside you probably said "move the scissors away." But where did that feeling come from? It came from learning — from rules people taught you, and from your own ability to notice when a situation is dangerous. Today we explore how you develop that incredibly important skill: recognizing and responding to unsafe situations before something goes wrong.

Safety isn't about being scared of everything. It's about being aware — noticing your environment and knowing what to do to protect yourself and others.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • C1.1 I can identify unsafe situations at home and at school.
  • C1.1 I can explain why safety rules exist and give examples of rules at home and school.
  • C1.1 I can demonstrate how to respond to an unsafe situation (tell a trusted adult).
  • C1.1 I can name at least three trusted adults I can go to when I feel unsafe.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

How to Introduce This Lesson

Walk through your home together and narrate your thinking: "I see the stove — that's a heat hazard, so our rule is always ask an adult before going near it." This makes safety thinking visible and natural rather than scary. Do the same for the child's school setting.

Key Teaching Moments

  • Unsafe ≠ scary: Distinguish between "dangerous" (requires immediate action) and "something to be careful about" (requires attention). Children who label everything "scary" often freeze rather than act.
  • Tell, don't handle alone: Reinforce that telling a trusted adult is the right response to an unsafe situation — it is not "tattling." Tattling is trying to get someone in trouble; telling is keeping people safe.
  • Trusted adults: Create a concrete list together. Tape it somewhere accessible. Include full names, roles, and if possible, phone numbers the child can find.

Questions to Ask

  • "Can you name three safety rules we have in our home? Why does each one exist?"
  • "If something felt unsafe at school and I wasn't there, who would you go to? Why them?"
  • "What would you do if you saw someone else getting hurt?"
Avoid: Framing safety as fear-based ("terrible things will happen if you don't follow rules"). Research shows fear-based safety education can increase anxiety without improving safety behaviours. Instead, frame safety as a superpower: "You are learning to be someone who protects yourself and others."

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Makes a Situation Unsafe?

A situation is unsafe when there is a chance that someone could get hurt. Sometimes the danger is physical (like a slippery floor or a sharp object). Sometimes the danger involves people (like someone asking you to do something that breaks your family's rules or makes you feel uncomfortable).

🏠 Did You Know? According to Health Canada, the home is one of the most common places where children aged 1–9 are injured. Falls, burns, cuts, and swallowing dangerous substances account for most home injuries in children — all of which safety rules help prevent.

Safety Rules at Home

  • Kitchen hazards: Hot stoves, sharp knives, heavy appliances. Rule: always have an adult present.
  • Electrical hazards: Outlets, cords, electronics near water. Rule: never touch outlets; keep electronics away from sinks and baths.
  • Stairs and heights: Falls are a leading cause of injury. Rule: hold the railing; never run on stairs.
  • Chemicals and medicines: Cleaning products, medicines, and some plants are poisonous if touched or swallowed. Rule: never touch or taste these without a trusted adult's permission and supervision.
  • Fire safety: Know your home's exit routes; never play with matches or lighters; know the sound of the smoke alarm.

Safety Rules at School

  • Walk, don't run, in hallways and on stairs.
  • Use equipment properly — never stand on swings or jump from high places.
  • Tell a teacher or adult if someone is hurt, sick, or being treated unkindly.
  • Know what to do in a fire drill — practise your class's procedure.
  • Never leave school property without telling your teacher.

Trusted Adults

A trusted adult is someone who cares about your safety and well-being. They are people in your life — not strangers — who you know well and who have shown you through their actions that they can be relied upon. Examples: parents/caregivers, grandparents, teachers, school principal, a neighbour your family knows well.

The golden rule: when something feels unsafe or wrong, tell a trusted adult right away. You are never in trouble for telling. Telling keeps you and others safe.

The phrase "stranger danger" is outdated. Modern safety education focuses on safe vs. unsafe behaviours and situations.

Key Teaching Moments

  • The "What if?" game: Practice scenarios together — "What if someone you didn't know said your mom sent them to pick you up?" (Rule: your family has a code word; if they don't know it, don't go.)
  • Asking strangers for help: Children need to know that in an emergency (lost, hurt, scared), it is safe to ask a stranger for help — ideally a store clerk, police officer, or a parent with children.
  • Road safety: Walk your usual routes with your child. Point out crosswalks, crossing guards, traffic signals. Practise the look-left-right-left routine together.

  • "What is our family's safety code word?"
  • "If you got lost at the mall, what would you do first? Second?"
  • "Can you show me the safest way to cross our street?"
Pro Tip: Establish a family code word that only you and your child know. If someone claims you sent them to pick up your child, they must know the code word. Change it if it is ever used or disclosed.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Strangers, Acquaintances, and Trusted Adults

  • A stranger is someone you do not know at all — you have never met them and your family does not know them.
  • An acquaintance is someone you've seen before or know by name, but don't know well.
  • A trusted adult is someone your family has approved and whom you can go to for help.

The rule is: you do not go anywhere with a stranger, you do not accept gifts from a stranger, and you tell a trusted adult if a stranger makes you feel uncomfortable.

Outdoor Safety Rules

  • Road safety: Always cross at crosswalks or corners. Look left, right, then left again before crossing. Make eye contact with drivers before crossing. Never assume a car has seen you.
  • Park and playground: Use equipment as designed. Tell a trusted adult where you are going. Go with a friend or group when possible.
  • If someone follows you: Walk quickly toward a busy, public place (store, library, school). Do not run to a hiding place alone.
  • Online and phone: Never share your full name, address, school name, or photos with someone you don't know in real life.

When to Ask a Stranger for Help

If you are lost, hurt, or scared and there is no trusted adult nearby, it is okay to ask a stranger for help — choosing whom to ask matters:

  • A police officer or security guard in uniform
  • A store clerk behind a counter
  • A parent with young children nearby

Approach them, use your voice, and say: "Excuse me, I need help. I am lost." You are in charge of choosing whom to ask.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Stages of Human Growth

  • Baby (0–1 year): Rapid physical growth. Learns to see, hear, roll over, sit, stand. Completely dependent on caregivers.
  • Toddler (1–3 years): Learns to walk, talk, and begin independent eating. Explores the world actively.
  • Early Childhood (3–6 years): Develops fine motor skills. Language skills explode. Begins to understand social rules. This is where you are now.
  • Middle Childhood (6–11 years): Physical strength and coordination increase. Logical thinking develops. Friendships become more important.
  • Adolescence (11–18 years): Puberty brings significant physical and emotional changes. Increasing independence.
  • Adulthood: Growth slows but continues — skills, wisdom, and experiences keep developing.
🦴 Did You Know? A baby is born with about 300 bones! Adults have only 206. As you grow, some bones fuse (join) together. Your skull alone starts as several separate pieces that gradually join into one solid bone — to protect your growing brain.

What Does Your Body Need to Grow?

  • Nutritious food: Provides the building blocks (proteins for muscles, calcium for bones, vitamins for everything).
  • Sleep: Growth hormone is released during sleep. Children aged 6–12 need 9–11 hours per night.
  • Physical activity: Strengthens muscles and bones, supports brain development, and improves mood.
  • Love and safety: Research shows that children who feel emotionally secure actually grow better. Stress hormones can literally slow growth if chronically elevated.
  • Fresh air and sunlight: Sunlight triggers your body to make Vitamin D, which is essential for strong bones.

All Bodies Are Different

There is no "right" way to look as a Grade 1 student. Some children are taller, some shorter. Some grow quickly, some more slowly. All of these variations are completely normal. Growth is not a race, and your body is not a competition. The only question to ask is: "Is my body healthy?" — not "Does my body look like someone else's?"

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Are Nutrients?

Nutrients are the parts of food your body actually uses. The main nutrient groups are:

  • Carbohydrates: Your body's main energy source. Found in bread, rice, pasta, oats, fruits, vegetables. Your brain runs almost entirely on glucose — which is why breakfast matters so much for school performance.
  • Proteins: Build and repair muscles, organs, and cells. Found in meat, fish, eggs, beans, lentils, tofu, dairy, nuts.
  • Fats: Essential for brain development, absorbing certain vitamins, and producing hormones. Healthy fats (fish, nuts, avocados, oils) are essential — not "bad."
  • Vitamins and minerals: Help hundreds of body processes. Calcium builds strong bones. Iron carries oxygen in blood. Vitamin C helps healing. Vitamin D supports bone growth.
  • Water: Your body is about 60% water. Children need about 5–7 cups per day.
🧠 Did You Know? Your brain is about 75% water. Even mild dehydration — losing just 1–2% of your body's water — can cause difficulty concentrating, headaches, and tiredness. Drinking enough water is one of the easiest things you can do to help your brain work better at school.

Canada's Food Guide

Canada's most recent food guide (updated in 2019) suggests filling your plate with:

  • Half vegetables and fruit — aim for colour and variety
  • Quarter whole grain foods — brown rice, whole wheat bread, oats, quinoa
  • Quarter protein foods — includes plant proteins (beans, lentils, tofu, nuts) and animal proteins (meat, fish, eggs, dairy)
  • Water as the drink of choice

The guide also emphasizes cooking at home, eating together, and enjoying food without being distracted by screens.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Hunger vs. Appetite

Hunger is a physical signal from your body that it needs fuel. Signs: stomach grumbling, feeling low-energy, difficulty concentrating, slight headache. Appetite is the desire to eat — often triggered by smell, sight, emotion, or habit, not by actual need. Understanding which one is driving the urge to eat helps you make informed choices.

Everyday Healthy Choices

  • Start with water: Before reaching for a snack, drink a glass of water. Thirst is often mistaken for hunger.
  • Choose whole foods when possible: An apple instead of apple-flavoured candy has more fibre, vitamins, and fewer added ingredients.
  • Eat mindfully: Sit down. Take smaller bites. Chew slowly. This gives your brain time to register fullness (it takes about 20 minutes).
  • Plan ahead: Having healthy snacks ready (fruit, cut vegetables, yogurt, nuts) means you're less likely to grab whatever's convenient when very hungry.
🎯 Did You Know? Research shows that when food is served on a larger plate, people tend to eat more — even if the food is the same amount. Our brains use visual cues to judge how much we "should" eat. This is why plate and portion size is studied by food scientists.

Understanding Food Advertising

Food companies spend billions of dollars trying to influence what you choose to eat. Common tactics:

  • Celebrity and character endorsements: A favourite character from a cartoon on the box makes children more likely to choose that cereal.
  • Emotional appeals: Ads show happy families, friendship, and success connected to their products.
  • Health buzzwords: "Natural," "organic," "superfood," "sugar-free" — these terms are sometimes meaningless or misleading.
  • Free toys and prizes: The product is secondary; the toy is the real attraction.

Being aware of these tactics makes you a more powerful decision-maker. Ask yourself: "Do I want this food because it's good for me, or because the ad made it look exciting?"

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Is Mental Health?

Mental health refers to your emotional, psychological, and social well-being. When your mental health is good, you can: think clearly, manage your feelings, build and maintain relationships, cope with challenges, and enjoy life. Mental health exists on a continuum — it changes from day to day and throughout life. Everyone has mental health, just as everyone has physical health.

🌍 Did You Know? According to the World Health Organization, mental health conditions affect 1 in 6 children aged 6–12 worldwide. In Canada, 70% of mental health problems start before age 18. This is why learning about mental health in Grade 1 is so important — early education is one of the best protective factors.

Understanding Emotions

An emotion is a felt response to something happening in your life or in your thoughts. Key emotions and their purposes:

  • Joy/Happiness: Signals that something is going well; reinforces behaviours worth repeating
  • Sadness: Signals loss or disappointment; encourages reflection and help-seeking
  • Fear/Anxiety: Warns of potential danger; prepares body to respond
  • Anger: Signals that something feels unfair or a boundary has been crossed; motivates action
  • Disgust: Protects from things that might be harmful
  • Surprise: Signals something unexpected; prompts quick assessment

All emotions serve a purpose. None of them are wrong to feel. What matters is how we respond to them.

What Supports My Mental Health?

  • Getting enough sleep (our brains process emotions during sleep)
  • Regular physical activity (exercise produces mood-lifting chemicals in the brain)
  • Strong connections with people who care about you
  • Time to play and do things you enjoy
  • Feeling safe at home and at school
  • Talking about how you feel instead of keeping feelings inside
  • Time in nature (research consistently shows nature reduces stress)

Calm Strategies — Managing Big Feelings

Feeling overwhelmed is a normal human experience. What sets people apart is not whether they feel intense emotions, but whether they have effective strategies for managing them. Today we build your personal toolkit.

C1.5 Mental Health

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

When something makes you very upset, have you ever done or said something you later wished you hadn't? What happened in your brain in that moment?

Neuroscientist Daniel Siegel describes what happens as "flipping your lid." Imagine your brain as your hand: your thumb is tucked in under your fingers when you're calm. But when emotions get very intense, the "lid" flips up — meaning the reasoning part of your brain has gone "offline." You literally cannot think as clearly. Knowing this helps you understand why calm-down strategies are so important: they help bring your lid back down.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

How to Introduce This Lesson

  • Create a calm-down space: Designate a comfortable corner of your home where your child can go when overwhelmed. Include soft cushions, favourite stuffed animal, calm activities. This is not a punishment — it is a tool.

  • "Which calm strategy feels most natural to you? Why do you think that one works for you?"
  • "Can you teach me your favourite calm strategy? I'd like to try it too."

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Happens When We're Overwhelmed

When you feel a very strong emotion, your body's alarm system activates. Your brain releases stress hormones (like adrenaline and cortisol). Your heart beats faster, your breathing quickens, your muscles tense. This "fight-flight-freeze" response is ancient and powerful. The good news: you can interrupt this response. The fastest way is through your breath.

Calming Strategies That Actually Work

  • Deep belly breathing (4-7-8 breathing): Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 7, breathe out for 8. The long exhale activates your body's "rest and calm" mode. Scientifically proven to lower heart rate within seconds.
  • Box breathing: Breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4. Used by military, athletes, and emergency workers worldwide.
  • 5-4-3-2-1 Grounding: Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste. Pulls your attention into the present moment and out of the emotional spiral.
  • Movement: Go for a walk, jump, shake your hands and arms vigorously. Physical movement burns off stress hormones.
  • Cold water: Splash cold water on your face or run cold water over your wrists. Triggers a dive reflex that slows heart rate.
  • Name it to tame it: Simply naming your emotion ("I am angry right now") activates the language centre of the brain, which calms the emotional centre.
🫁 Did You Know? Your breath is the only automatic body function that you can also control voluntarily. You can't consciously control your heartbeat or digestion — but you can control your breath. This makes breathing the direct lever to your nervous system. Athletes use this before competition; surgeons use it before operations.

🔬 Section 5 — Think Like a Scientist

Breath Experiment: Try this experiment to measure the effect of breathing on how you feel:

  1. Sit still for 2 minutes and breathe normally. Note how you feel.
  2. Now breathe in as quickly and shallowly as possible for 1 minute (like panting). How do you feel?
  3. Take 5 slow deep breaths (4 counts in, 6 counts out). How do you feel now?
  4. Repeat the slow breathing 3 more times. What do you notice?

Record your observations. This demonstrates the direct link between breathing and nervous system state.

For each strategy, write: the name of the strategy, step-by-step instructions (as if explaining to someone who has never heard of it), when you would use it (what kind of situation?), and whether you've used it before and how well it worked.

  3. You really wanted to win a game, but you lost and it feels deeply unfair.

STRAND C · LESSON 8

Medicines & Substances — What You Need to Know

Medicines help people feel better and fight illness — but they are powerful substances that must be used carefully. Understanding the difference between safe use and harmful misuse is an essential life skill.

C1.3 Substances

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

How does a pill know where to go inside your body to make you feel better?

This is actually a fascinating question that chemists and pharmacologists spend careers answering. Medicines are designed to interact with specific parts of your body — but they travel through your entire bloodstream to get there, which is why medicines always affect more than just the one part you want to fix. And it's also why taking the wrong medicine, the wrong amount, or medicine not prescribed for you can be very dangerous. Today we learn the essential rules about medicines and substances.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • C1.3 I can explain what medicines are and why they must only be taken with adult supervision.
  • C1.3 I can describe the rules for safe use of medicines (right person, right medicine, right amount, right time).
  • C1.3 I can identify common household substances that are dangerous if misused.
  • C1.3 I can explain what to do if I accidentally touch or swallow something dangerous.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

How to Introduce This Lesson

Use real medicine bottles as teaching tools. Show your child: the label (name, dosage, instructions, expiry date), the child-safety cap, why it is stored out of reach. Explain: "This medicine is only for me, only at this dose, only at these times. Even though it helps me, the same amount could hurt you because you are smaller."

Key Teaching Moments

  • Medicine is not candy: Some children's medicines are flavoured to be palatable. Emphasize clearly: taking more than the prescribed dose is never okay, even if it tastes good.
  • Household hazards: Walk through your home and identify substances that should be stored safely: cleaning products, laundry pods, alcohol, certain plants. Laundry pods are particularly dangerous and account for thousands of poisoning calls per year in North America.
  • Poison Control: Post the Ontario Poison Control Centre number prominently: 1-800-268-9017. Teach your child: if anyone swallows something dangerous, tell an adult immediately; if no adult is there, call 9-1-1.

Questions to Ask

  • "What do you do if you find medicine somewhere and you're not sure what it is?"
  • "Why is it important to always take the exact amount the label says — not more?"
  • "Where are the medications in our home stored? Are they out of reach of younger children?"
Important: The Ontario Poison Control Centre number is 1-800-268-9017. Program it into your phone and post it in a visible location at home. In a genuine emergency, call 9-1-1 first.

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

What Are Medicines?

Medicines are substances developed by scientists and approved by government health agencies to help treat illness, relieve pain, fight infection, or prevent disease. They are powerful tools that are only safe when used correctly:

  • Right person: Only take medicine that was prescribed for you.
  • Right medicine: Only take what a doctor, pharmacist, or trusted adult has given you for your specific condition.
  • Right amount: The dose on the label matters. Taking more does not make you get better faster — it can make you seriously ill.
  • Right time: Follow instructions exactly (with food, before bed, every 8 hours).
  • With adult supervision: Children should never take medicine on their own, even if they've taken it before.
💊 Did You Know? Over 90% of children's medicine calls to Poison Control centres involve a child who found medicine that was not properly stored or locked away. Childproof caps slow children down — they do not stop them. The safest storage is out of sight and out of reach, in a locked cabinet.

Dangerous Substances at Home

Many common household products are dangerous if touched, swallowed, or inhaled: cleaning products (bleach, oven cleaner), laundry pods, pest control products, car products (antifreeze tastes sweet but is highly poisonous), and certain plants. The rule: if you find any of these, do not touch, smell, or taste them. Tell a trusted adult immediately.

What to Do in an Emergency

  1. Stay calm.
  2. Tell an adult immediately.
  3. If no adult is available, call 9-1-1.
  4. Do NOT make the person vomit unless a medical professional tells you to.
  5. Have the container ready to show emergency responders.

🔬 Section 5 — Think Like a Scientist

Is it locked? Is it out of reach of young children? Identify any storage improvements that could be made. Discuss: What would you do if you found one of these substances that had been left out?

Make it clear enough so even a younger sibling could understand it.

  2. You find some pills on the floor that fell out of a bottle. They look like candy. No adult is home.
  3. Your little brother drinks something from under the sink. He starts crying and his mouth hurts. Your parent is in the backyard.

  • What is the difference between a drug and a medicine? When does a substance cross from one to the other?

STRAND C · LESSON 9

Our Environment & Healthy Living

The environment you live in — the air, water, soil, and green spaces around you — has a profound effect on your health. And your choices have an effect on the environment. This connection runs both ways, and understanding it helps you be a healthier, more conscious citizen.

C1.6 Environmental Health

🌟 Section 1 — Wonder Hook

If you lived in a city with heavy pollution, how might that affect your body over 10 years — even if you felt fine right now?

This is a question environmental health scientists study, and the answers are striking. The quality of the air you breathe, the water you drink, the noise levels in your neighbourhood, and access to green spaces all measurably affect your lungs, heart, brain, sleep, and mental health — often in ways you cannot see or feel until years later. Today we explore how your environment shapes your health, and how you can help shape a healthier environment for everyone.

🎯 Section 2 — Learning Objectives

  • C1.6 I can explain how the environment affects human health.
  • C1.6 I can identify at least 4 environmental factors that affect health (air quality, water, green space, noise).
  • C1.6 I can describe actions I can take at home and school to protect environmental health.
  • C1.6 I can explain the connection between protecting nature and protecting human health.

👩‍🏫 Section 3 — Parent Teaching Guide

How to Introduce This Lesson

Take a walk together and narrate what you notice about the environment: "That plant cleans the air. That factory might produce pollution. That park gives people mental health benefits." Connecting abstract environmental concepts to the immediately observable world makes them real and memorable.

Key Teaching Moments

  • Local before global: Start with your immediate environment — your home's air quality, your neighbourhood's green space, your local water supply. Then expand outward.
  • Hope, not despair: Environmental education should empower, not frighten. Emphasize what individuals, communities, and governments can and do change. Children are among the most effective environmental advocates when they feel empowered.
  • Canada's context: Canada has some of the world's cleanest water and air — and it requires active protection to stay that way. Ontario's environmental regulations, Indigenous land stewardship, and community conservation efforts are worth discussing.

Questions to Ask

  • "Can you name three ways our choices at home affect the environment outside?"
  • "What does our neighbourhood have that helps people stay healthy? What is missing?"
  • "What is one thing our family could do this week that would be better for the environment?"

📖 Section 4 — The Lesson

Air Quality

The air you breathe enters your lungs with every breath — about 20,000 times per day. Polluted air can cause or worsen: asthma, allergies, lung disease, heart disease, and can even affect brain development in children. The good news: trees and plants filter air pollution. More green spaces = cleaner air.

🌳 Did You Know? A single mature tree can absorb about 48 pounds (22 kg) of carbon dioxide per year and produce enough oxygen for two human beings. Toronto's urban forest — over 10 million trees — provides measurable air quality, mental health, and cooling benefits to the city. Trees are a technology.

Water Quality

Access to clean water is fundamental to health. Contaminated water can cause serious illness. In Canada, most tap water is safe and rigorously tested — but some Indigenous communities still do not have reliable access to clean drinking water, an ongoing public health issue worth knowing about.

Green Space and Nature

Research from environmental psychology consistently shows that time in nature reduces stress hormones, lowers blood pressure, improves mood, boosts creativity, and strengthens the immune system. Children who have regular access to green space have better attention spans and lower rates of anxiety. Parks, forests, rivers, and gardens are not luxuries — they are health infrastructure.

Noise Pollution

Chronic loud noise (near highways, airports, construction) is a health hazard. It disrupts sleep, increases stress hormones, and can damage hearing with long-term exposure. Even moderate noise during sleep can reduce sleep quality without people realizing it.

What You Can Do

  • Reduce, reuse, recycle — lessens waste going to landfills
  • Walk, bike, or use public transit when possible — reduces air pollution
  • Turn off lights and electronics when not in use — reduces energy use
  • Avoid single-use plastics — protect waterways and oceans
  • Grow plants — even one indoor plant improves air quality and mental health
  • Participate in community clean-ups — protect local green spaces and waterways

  2. Air quality: Do you smell any pollution? See any exhaust? Rate 1–5.
  3. Noise: How loud is your neighbourhood? Traffic, construction? Rate 1–5.
  4. Water access: Are there water fountains? Evidence of clean streams or waterways?
  5. Litter: Is the neighbourhood clean? Rate 1–5.

Write a short "environmental health report" for your neighbourhood: What is doing well? What could be improved?
