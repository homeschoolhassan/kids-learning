Strand B: Number · Lesson 1

How Far Can We Count?

B1.1 · B1.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🌟 Look around your house right now. Can you find something you can count? Pick a jar of buttons, a box of crayons, or a bag of dried pasta pieces.

How many do you think are in there? Write your guess before you count — there is no wrong guess!

The Big Question: How far can we count — and does the order of numbers ever change?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Count forward from any number up to 50.
  • Count backward from any number within 50.
  • Say and write number words from one to twenty.
  • Identify the number that comes before and after any given number.
  • Represent amounts up to 50 using objects, drawings, or numbers.

B1.1 B1.2

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Sit together with a collection of small objects (buttons, blocks, pasta pieces). Push them into a pile.

  1. Say: "Let's count these out loud together." Touch each object as you count.
  2. Ask: "How many did we find? Let's count again to check."
  3. Move some away. Ask: "Now how many are left?"
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Always touch each object as you count — this builds one-to-one correspondence, the foundation of all counting.

Draw a number line from 0 to 20 on a large piece of paper or use masking tape on the floor.

  1. Call out a number: "Stand on 7!" Child hops to it.
  2. Ask: "What number comes right before 7? Right after 7?"
  3. Count backward from 15 to 0 together, stepping back on each count.
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Backward counting is harder than forward — do it slowly and cheerfully. Mistakes are just detours, not dead ends.

Use flashcards or write on paper. Show the numeral and the word side-by-side.

  • one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten
  • eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty

Ask child to match number words to numerals. Clap the syllables in each word.

👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Put number word cards on a 'word wall' in your learning space. Your child will read them naturally throughout the day.

Place 50 small objects in a bag. Have child count them out in groups of 10.

  1. Count 10 → put in a cup. Count 10 more → second cup. Continue to 50.
  2. Ask: "How many cups do we have? How many in each cup? How many altogether?"
  3. Write the number 50 together and count backward from 50.
🌱 Grouping by 10s builds the foundation for place value in Lesson B-3!

Show a number (e.g., 23) and ask your child to represent it in THREE ways:

  • 🟤 With objects (arrange 23 buttons)
  • ✏️ With a drawing (tally marks or dots)
  • 🔢 With the numeral (write "23")
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Multiple representations of the same number teach your child that numbers are flexible ideas, not just symbols.
📖

The Lesson

Numbers are labels for how many. When we count, we follow a strict order — the same order every time. Let's explore.

📌 The Counting Rules:
1. Say one number for each object (one-to-one).
2. Use the number words in order.
3. The last number you say IS the total.
Count and Sort

Get 30 small objects. Count them out loud, touching each one.

  • How many did you count?
  • Now sort them into groups of 5. How many groups?
  • Try counting by 5s: 5, 10, 15…
Before and After

Your parent says a number. You say the number that comes RIGHT BEFORE and RIGHT AFTER.

  • Before and after 12 → ___ and ___
  • Before and after 29 → ___ and ___
  • Before and after 49 → ___ and ___
Number Word Match

Read each number word and write the numeral:

  • fourteen → ___
  • twenty → ___
  • eleven → ___
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

Mathematicians always ask WHY, not just HOW.

  1. Attempt: Count 37 objects and write the number.
  2. Think: Could you count them in a different order and get a different answer? Try it!
  3. Explain: Tell your parent what you discovered.
  4. Verify: Count them one more time to confirm.
🤯 The order you COUNT objects does not change the TOTAL. This is called the Commutative Property of Counting. Isn't that amazing?

Strand B: Number · Lesson 2

Seeing Numbers at a Glance

B1.3
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🎲 Stare at a die for one second, then look away. How many dots did you see?

You didn't have to count one by one — you just knew. How is that possible?

The Big Question: Can our brains recognise numbers without counting?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Instantly recognise quantities up to 6 without counting.
  • Identify quantities up to 10 by recognising smaller groups.
  • Explain how you see groups inside a larger number.

B1.3

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Make dot cards (index cards with sticker dots or drawn dots, arranged like a die face). Use quantities 1–6.

  1. Flash a card for 2 seconds. Ask: "How many? Don't count — just look!"
  2. Child says the number. Reveal card again to confirm.
  3. Ask: "How did you know? What did you see?"
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Subitizing is a real mathematical skill. The brain is pattern-matching, not counting. Praise any correct instant recognition warmly.

Roll two dice. Before counting dots, guess the total just by looking.

  • Roll a 3 and a 4: Can you see 7 without adding?
  • Arrange 6 objects in different patterns. Which pattern is easiest to recognise instantly?
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Research shows children who subitize well develop stronger number sense and mental math skills later. This is one of the most powerful early math skills.

Show 8 dots arranged as two groups of 4. Ask:

  • "How many do you see in this group? And this group?"
  • "So how many altogether?"
  • "Can you show me another way to see 8?" (e.g., 5 + 3)
🌱 When children see '5 and 3 makes 8' they are already learning addition!
📖

The Lesson

Subitizing means seeing HOW MANY without counting. It is how our brain naturally works with small numbers.

🧠 You can subitize quantities up to about 5 or 6 instantly. For larger numbers, your brain breaks them into smaller groups — this is called perceptual subitizing vs. conceptual subitizing.
Dot Pattern Practice

Look at each set of dots for only 2 seconds. Then cover it up and write how many you saw.

  • ● ● ● (3 dots in a row)
  • ● ● / ● ● (2 dots on top, 2 on bottom)
  • ● ● ● / ● ● (3 on top, 2 on bottom)

Did you count, or did you just know?

Make Your Own Dot Cards

Draw dot cards for numbers 1 through 10. Arrange the dots in patterns that are easy to recognise.

Share them with a family member and test them!

🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

Think about WHY certain dot patterns are easier to recognise than others.

  1. Attempt: Arrange 7 dots two different ways. Which is easier to subitize?
  2. Think: What makes one pattern better than another?
  3. Explain: Tell your parent your best theory.
  4. Verify: Test your theory on a family member!
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: There is no wrong answer here — exploring patterns IS the mathematics!
✏️

Strand B: Number · Lesson 3

Tens and Ones — The Power of Place Value

B2.1 · B2.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🏗️ Imagine you have 34 LEGO bricks. Would you carry them one by one, or in groups?

Numbers work the same way! The number 34 is not just "thirty-four things" — it is a package.

The Big Question: How does bundling numbers into groups of 10 make everything easier?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Understand that 10 ones = 1 ten.
  • Decompose two-digit numbers into tens and ones.
  • Use base-ten blocks or drawings to represent two-digit numbers.
  • Read and write two-digit numbers.

B2.1 B2.2

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Use straws, popsicle sticks, or pencils. Count out 10 and bundle them with a rubber band or pipe cleaner.

  1. Make 3 bundles of 10. Lay them out. Ask: "How many bundles? How many in each? How many sticks altogether?"
  2. Add 7 loose sticks. Ask: "Now how many altogether? How do you know?"
  3. Write: 3 tens + 7 ones = 37.
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Use real objects for this — DO NOT rush to written numerals. The physical bundling is the learning.

Draw a T-chart: TENS | ONES. Give child a two-digit number and have them fill in the chart.

  • 25 → 2 tens, 5 ones
  • 40 → 4 tens, 0 ones
  • 13 → ___ tens, ___ ones
⚠️ 40 has ZERO ones — this trips many children up. Make sure to address this explicitly!

Show that 45 = 40 + 5. Say: "The 4 means 4 tens, which equals 40. The 5 means 5 ones."

Practice with: 32, 18, 50, 27, 49

👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Connect this to money: a ten-dollar bill = 10 loonies. This makes place value tangible.
📖

The Lesson

Our number system groups things into tens. This makes large numbers easier to read, say, and work with.

📌 Place Value: The position (place) of a digit tells its value.
In 47: the 4 is in the TENS place (= 40), the 7 is in the ONES place (= 7).
Build the Number

Use objects to build these numbers with tens and ones:

  • 36 = ___ tens and ___ ones
  • 52 = ___ tens and ___ ones
  • 14 = ___ tens and ___ ones
Expanded Form

Write in expanded form (e.g., 45 = 40 + 5):

  • 23 = ___ + ___
  • 48 = ___ + ___
  • 30 = ___ + ___
Mystery Number

Read the clue and write the number:

  • I have 3 tens and 8 ones. I am: ___
  • I have 5 tens and 0 ones. I am: ___
  • I have 2 tens and 9 ones. I am: ___
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

  1. Attempt: The number 63 — draw it using tens and ones.
  2. Think: If I give away 1 ten from 63, what number do I have?
  3. Explain: Why does changing the tens digit change the number more than changing the ones digit?
  4. Verify: Use objects to check your thinking.
💡 The digit in the TENS place is worth 10 times as much as the same digit in the ONES place. That is the power of place value!
✏️

Strand B: Number · Lesson 4

Comparing and Ordering Numbers

B1.4
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

⚖️ You and your friend both have a bag of marbles. How do you find out who has more — without counting every single one?

What if you could just LOOK and know? Or use a secret strategy?

The Big Question: How do mathematicians compare numbers quickly and accurately?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Compare two numbers up to 50 using greater than, less than, and equal to.
  • Use the symbols >, <, and = correctly.
  • Order a set of numbers from least to greatest and greatest to least.
  • Explain the reasoning behind a comparison.

B1.4

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Draw a crocodile mouth. The crocodile always faces the BIGGER number because it wants to eat more food.

  • Write: 15 ___ 23. Ask child which way the crocodile faces. Write <.
  • Write: 38 ___ 21. Child places crocodile. Write >.
  • Write: 17 ___ 17. Both amounts equal — crocodile can't choose! Write =.
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: The crocodile is a memory hook, not a mathematical rule. Ultimately, teach the REASON: check tens first, then ones.

Compare 43 and 38. Walk through:

  1. Look at the TENS digit first. 43 has 4 tens. 38 has 3 tens.
  2. 4 tens > 3 tens, so 43 > 38. Done!
  3. When tens are equal (e.g., 25 vs. 27): look at the ONES. 5 < 7, so 25 < 27.
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: This strategy prepares children for comparing multi-digit numbers in later grades. Practice it explicitly.

Write 5 numbers on cards: 14, 32, 7, 28, 45. Ask child to arrange them from smallest to largest.

  • Ask: "How did you decide where to put each number?"
  • Now reverse: largest to smallest.
  • Ask: "Which number is in the middle?"
📖

The Lesson

When comparing numbers, always start with the largest place value — the tens digit first, then ones.

📌 Symbols:
> means "greater than" (e.g., 7 > 4)
< means "less than" (e.g., 3 < 9)
= means "equal to" (e.g., 12 = 12)
Compare These Pairs

Fill in >, <, or = :

  • 24 ___ 42
  • 35 ___ 35
  • 19 ___ 21
  • 40 ___ 38
Order the Numbers

Write these numbers from LEAST to GREATEST:

  • 17, 5, 32, 28, 11 → ___
  • 45, 9, 22, 38, 16 → ___
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

  1. Attempt: Put these in order from greatest to least: 29, 41, 7, 38, 15
  2. Think: What would change if you added the number 30 to this list?
  3. Explain: How do you know 41 is bigger than 29 without counting to both?
  4. Verify: Draw both numbers using tens and ones to confirm.
✏️

Adding Up — Joining Numbers to 20

B2.3 · B2.4
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🍎 You have 7 apples in one basket and 6 in another. A friend wants to know the total — but you can't pour them together. What do you do?

The Big Question: When we join two groups, how do we find the new total quickly and confidently?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Add two numbers whose sum is up to 20.
  • Use multiple strategies: counting on, making 10, using doubles.
  • Write and solve addition number sentences (equations).
  • Solve simple addition word problems.

B2.3 B2.4

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Start with the larger number, then count on.

  • 9 + 4: Start at 9, count on four: 10, 11, 12, 13. Answer: 13.
  • Practice: 8+3, 7+5, 6+4
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Hold up fingers to count on — this scaffolds the strategy physically before internalizing it.

Use a ten frame (a 2×5 grid drawn on paper). Fill it, then count the overflow.

  • 8 + 5: Fill 8 spots on frame. Need 2 more to make 10. That uses 2 of the 5. Left: 3. So 10 + 3 = 13.
🌟 Making 10 is the most powerful addition strategy. It works because of how our number system is built.
  • Doubles: 6+6=12, 7+7=14 (use fingers, then memorize)
  • Near-doubles: 6+7 = 6+6+1 = 13
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Doubles are the most memorized addition facts — once your child knows them, near-doubles come automatically.
📖

The Lesson

Addition means putting groups together to find the total.

📌 The Equation: 7 + 5 = 12
7 and 5 are addends. 12 is the sum.
Strategy Practice

Solve each problem. Write WHICH strategy you used: Counting On / Making 10 / Doubles.

  • 9 + 6 = ___ (strategy: ___)
  • 8 + 8 = ___ (strategy: ___)
  • 7 + 3 = ___ (strategy: ___)
  • 6 + 7 = ___ (strategy: ___)
Word Problems

Read, draw a picture, then solve:

  • There are 9 red fish and 5 blue fish. How many fish in all?
  • Amara has 8 stickers. She gets 4 more. How many does she have now?
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

  1. Attempt: Solve 8 + 7 three different ways.
  2. Think: Do all three ways give the same answer? Why?
  3. Explain: Which strategy is fastest for you? Why?
  4. Verify: Check with counting objects.
💡 The ORDER of the addends doesn't change the sum: 8+7 = 7+8. This is called the Commutative Property!

Strand B: Number · Lesson 6

Taking Away — Subtraction to 20

B2.5 · B2.6
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🍪 There were 14 cookies on the plate. Now there are 6. What happened to the missing cookies?!

Subtraction is the story of WHAT IS LEFT — and also the story of what is MISSING or DIFFERENT.

The Big Question: When something goes away or is missing, how do we figure out the number?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Subtract numbers up to 20 using multiple strategies.
  • Understand subtraction as taking away, finding a difference, or completing a missing part.
  • Write and solve subtraction equations.
  • Recognise the relationship between addition and subtraction (fact families).

B2.5 B2.6

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Use 15 blocks. Remove 6. Ask: "How many are left?" Count what remains. Write: 15 − 6 = 9.

👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Let child physically take away objects before moving to numerals. The action comes first, the symbol second.

Count backward to subtract. 13 − 4: start at 13, count back 4 steps: 12, 11, 10, 9. Answer: 9.

  • Practice: 15−3, 11−5, 14−2
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Use a number line drawn on paper — child puts finger on the starting number and hops backward.

Show that 8 + 7 = 15 means we also know 15 − 7 = 8 and 15 − 8 = 7.

Write the FACT FAMILY TRIANGLE: put 15 at top, 8 and 7 at corners.

🌟 Knowing fact families means one memory does the work of four: two addition facts and two subtraction facts!
📖

The Lesson

Subtraction has THREE meanings:

📌 1. Take Away: 12 birds, 4 fly away → 12 − 4 = 8 left
2. Compare: 15 crayons vs 9 crayons → difference = 15 − 9 = 6
3. Missing Part: ___ + 6 = 14 → 14 − 6 = 8
Three Stories

Write THREE different subtraction word problems — one for each meaning (take away, compare, missing part). Solve each one.

Fact Families

Write the full fact family for each set of numbers:

  • Numbers: 6, 8, 14 → write all 4 equations
  • Numbers: 7, 9, 16 → write all 4 equations
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

  1. Attempt: Solve 16 − 9 without counting every number.
  2. Think: Can you use your addition knowledge? (What + 9 = 16?)
  3. Explain: Why is addition connected to subtraction?
  4. Verify: Check with objects.
💡 Subtraction and addition are inverse operations — they undo each other. Every subtraction problem is secretly an addition problem!
✏️

Subitize
Instantly know how many without counting one by one.
e.g. See ●●● and immediately know it's 3.

One of the symbols 0–9 used to write numbers.
e.g. The number 47 has two digits: 4 and 7.
Place Value
The value of a digit based on its position in a number.
e.g. In 34, the 3 means 30 (three tens).
Tens
Groups of 10 ones bundled together.
e.g. 3 tens = 30.
Ones
Single units; the digits from 0–9 in the ones place.
e.g. In 27, there are 7 ones.
Expanded Form
Writing a number as a sum of its place values.
e.g. 45 = 40 + 5.
Greater Than
A number that is larger than another; symbol: >.
e.g. 12 > 9.
Less Than
A number that is smaller than another; symbol: <.
e.g. 5 < 11.
Equal To
Two amounts that are exactly the same; symbol: =.
e.g. 7 + 3 = 10.
Addend
A number added to another number.
e.g. In 6 + 4, both 6 and 4 are addends.
Sum
The total when two or more numbers are added.
e.g. 6 + 4 = 10; the sum is 10.
Difference
The result of subtracting one number from another.
e.g. 15 − 8 = 7; the difference is 7.
Commutative Property
Adding in any order gives the same sum.
e.g. 3 + 7 = 7 + 3.
Fraction
A number representing part of a whole divided into equal parts.
e.g. ½ = one of two equal pieces.
Numerator
The top number in a fraction — parts you have.
e.g. In ¾, the numerator is 3.
Denominator
The bottom number in a fraction — total equal parts.
e.g. In ¾, the denominator is 4.

C2.1 · C2.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

⚖️ Imagine a perfectly balanced see-saw. If you add one more person to the left side, what happens? What do you need to do to the RIGHT side to make it balance again?

The Big Question: What does the equals sign (=) REALLY mean — and is it more than just "the answer goes here"?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Understand the equals sign as meaning "the same as" or "balanced."
  • Identify and write true equations using addition and subtraction to 20.
  • Determine whether an equation is true or false.
  • Find missing values in equations on both sides of the equals sign.

C2.1 C2.2

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Make a simple balance from a ruler balanced on a pencil, or use a real scale.

  1. Put 8 objects on the left. Ask: "What do we put on the right to balance it?"
  2. Show that 5+3 on the left = 8 on the right. Write: 5+3 = 8.
  3. Ask: "What if I put 4+4 on the right instead? Still balanced?" Write: 5+3 = 4+4.
⚠️ The key insight: = means BALANCE, not 'what comes next.' 5+3 = 4+4 is a valid equation! This surprises many children (and some adults).

Write equations and ask: True or False?

  • 6 + 4 = 10 → True (balanced)
  • 7 + 3 = 11 → False (not balanced)
  • 8 = 5 + 3 → True! (the number can be on the left!)
  • 9 + 1 = 6 + 4 → True!
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Many children think = always means 'put the answer after it.' The equation 8 = 3+5 confuses them. Make sure to flip equations so child is comfortable with any arrangement.

Introduce equations with blanks: 7 + ___ = 12

  1. Think: "What makes this balance?"
  2. Strategy: 12 − 7 = 5, so the missing number is 5.
  3. Try: ___ + 4 = 9, 15 − ___ = 6, 8 + 3 = ___
📖

The Lesson

The equals sign (=) is one of the most important symbols in all of mathematics. It means "is the same as" or "is balanced with."

📌 Right side and left side of = must always have the same VALUE — even if they look different.
5 + 5 = 4 + 6 = 8 + 2 = 10 — all balanced!

  • 7 + 8 = 14: ___
  • 6 = 3 + 3: ___
  • 9 + 1 = 5 + 5: ___
  • 11 = 12 − 1: ___

  • 5 + ___ = 11

C3.1 · C3.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

The Big Question: How do we write instructions SO PERFECTLY that a robot (or computer) never makes mistakes?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Understand what a sequence of instructions (algorithm) is.
  • Write step-by-step instructions to accomplish a simple task.
  • Identify and correct errors (bugs) in a sequence of instructions.
  • Act out (run) simple coded instructions.

C3.1 C3.2

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Tell your child: "I am a robot. I can only follow EXACT instructions." Then do exactly what they say — even if it leads somewhere silly.

  1. Child's goal: "Walk to the kitchen and open the fridge."
  2. If they say "walk forward" you walk forward (even into a wall unless they say "turn").
  3. Debrief: "What instructions were missing? How could we make it perfect?"
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: This activity is called an 'unplugged' coding activity — it teaches computational thinking without any device.

Task: write a step-by-step algorithm (list of instructions) to make a peanut butter sandwich.

  • Step 1: Get 2 slices of bread.
  • Step 2: Open the peanut butter jar.
  • Step 3: ...

Then FOLLOW the algorithm exactly. What did you forget? That's called a bug.

🐛 A 'bug' in code is an error in the instructions. The word 'bug' came from a real moth that got stuck inside an early computer and caused it to malfunction!

Give child a broken algorithm (with steps in wrong order or missing). Have them find and fix the bugs.

Example broken algorithm for 'wash your hands':

  1. Dry your hands with a towel.
  2. Turn on the tap.
  3. Apply soap and rub.
  4. Rinse off the soap.

What is wrong? Fix it!

📖

The Lesson

A program is a list of step-by-step instructions for a computer to follow. Each step is called a command. Together, the steps form an algorithm.

📌 Algorithm Rules:
1. Each step must be CLEAR and SPECIFIC.
2. The steps must be in the RIGHT ORDER.
3. No important step can be missing.

Test it: have your parent follow your algorithm EXACTLY. Did it work?

Mathematical Modelling — Math in the Real World

C4.1
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🌍 Every day, meteorologists (weather scientists) look at real weather data and make a MODEL to predict tomorrow's weather. They're not always right — but their models make them far better than random guessing.

The Big Question: How can math help us understand and predict real things in the world?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Understand that a mathematical model represents a real situation.
  • Use math (counting, patterns, equations) to describe and solve real-world problems.
  • Evaluate whether a model fits a situation well.
  • Apply the 4-step modelling cycle: Understand → Analyse → Create → Assess.

C4.1

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Show child a map of your neighbourhood. Ask: "Is this map the real neighbourhood? How is it the same? How is it different?"

  • A map is a MODEL — a simpler version of reality that helps us understand or navigate.
  • A math model uses numbers and equations to represent a real situation.
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Children naturally make models: toy cars, dollhouses, drawings. Connect this natural activity to mathematical modelling.

Work through a real problem together using the 4 steps:

Problem: We want to plant 24 seeds in rows. How many rows could we make?

  1. Understand: What do we know? (24 seeds) What do we need? (rows)
  2. Analyse: What math can help? (patterns, equal groups)
  3. Create: Build the model: rows of 6 = 4 rows; rows of 4 = 6 rows; rows of 8 = 3 rows.
  4. Assess: Do any answers fit the real garden better? (What size row is practical?)

Give child a real-world scenario: "Our family needs to buy enough juice boxes for a party of 15 kids, and juice comes in packs of 4. How many packs?"

Have them work through all 4 steps. Focus especially on Step 4: Does the math model work in reality? (You can't buy 3.75 packs — you need to round up!)

🌟 Mathematical modelling is what engineers, doctors, architects, and economists do every day. Your child is practising a real professional skill!
📖

The Lesson

When we use math to understand or solve a REAL problem, we are creating a mathematical model.

📌 The 4 Steps of Mathematical Modelling:
1. Understand: What do I know? What do I need to find?
2. Analyse: What math tools can help?
3. Create: Build the model (equation, drawing, table, pattern).
4. Assess: Does my model make sense in real life?
Model This!

Use the 4-step model to solve this real problem:

"You want to share 18 stickers equally among 3 friends. How many does each friend get?"

  1. Understand: What do you know? ___
  2. Analyse: What math can help? ___
  3. Create: Write the equation or draw the model: ___
  4. Assess: Does the answer make sense? ___

📚 Vocabulary Panel

Key words for this strand — read, discuss, and use them!
Pattern
Something that repeats in a predictable, organized way.
e.g. Red, blue, red, blue is a pattern.
Core
The smallest part of a repeating pattern that keeps coming back.
e.g. In ABABAB, the core is AB.
Repeating Pattern
A pattern where the same core unit repeats over and over.
e.g. Circle, square, circle, square…
Growing Pattern
A pattern where each term gets larger by the same amount.
e.g. 2, 4, 6, 8 (grows by 2 each time).
Shrinking Pattern
A pattern where each term gets smaller by the same amount.
e.g. 20, 15, 10, 5 (shrinks by 5).
Rule
The instruction that tells how a pattern changes from one term to the next.
e.g. The rule is 'add 3 each time.'
Equals Sign (=)
A symbol meaning both sides have the same value or are balanced.
e.g. 6 + 4 = 10 means 10 = 10.
Equation
A mathematical statement showing two things are equal.
e.g. 5 + 7 = 12.
Unknown
A missing number in an equation, shown by a □ or blank.
e.g. □ + 6 = 11 — find □.
Algorithm
A step-by-step set of instructions to accomplish a task.
e.g. A recipe is an everyday algorithm.
Bug
An error in a set of instructions (code) that causes the wrong result.
e.g. The robot walked into a wall because of a bug.
Mathematical Model
Using math (numbers, equations, drawings) to represent a real situation.
e.g. Writing 5 × 4 = 20 to plan seating at 5 tables of 4.
Variable
A symbol (like a box or letter) representing an unknown or changeable quantity.
e.g. In □ + 5 = 12, □ is a variable.
Term
Each number or item in a pattern or sequence.
e.g. In 2, 4, 6, each number is a term.

The Big Question: When you sort things into groups, what rules are you actually using — and can the SAME object belong to more than one group?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Sort objects into groups based on one or more attributes.
  • Explain the sorting rule used.
  • Recognise that the same objects can be sorted in different ways.
  • Compare groups after sorting (more, fewer, equal).

D1.1

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Collect 20 mixed objects. Ask child to sort them by ONE attribute.

  • Colour, shape, size, material, whether it bounces, whether it's living...

Ask: "How did you decide? Can you explain your rule?"

👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Don't suggest the sorting rule — let the child choose. Any consistent rule is mathematically valid!

Take the same 20 objects. Ask child to sort them a DIFFERENT way.

  • First sort: by colour. Second sort: by size. Third sort: by material.

Key question: "Did the objects change? What changed?" (Only the rule changed!)

💡 The same data can be organised many different ways. This is one of the most important ideas in data literacy — and in science!

After sorting, count each group. Write: Group A: ___, Group B: ___, Group C: ___

  • Which group has the most? The least? Are any groups equal?
  • Ask: "Can one object belong to TWO groups at the same time?"

The Lesson

When we sort, we put objects into groups based on something they have in common — this is called an attribute.

📌 Attributes are characteristics of objects:
colour · shape · size · texture · material · function · number of sides

For each sort: count each group and record the number.

D1.4
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

📊 Imagine you collected data about favourite animals: 7 people said dog, 3 said cat, 5 said bird.

Which is easier to understand — those three numbers, or a picture that shows all three at once?

The Big Question: Why do we turn numbers into pictures — and what can you SEE in a graph that's hard to see in a list of numbers?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Organise and display data using concrete graphs (using real objects).
  • Create and read a simple pictograph.
  • Use a pictograph to answer questions (most, fewest, how many more).
  • Understand that each picture in a pictograph represents one item.

D1.4

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Make a physical graph using real objects on the floor or table.

  1. Collect 20 objects of 3-4 types (red blocks, blue blocks, green blocks).
  2. Line them up in rows (or columns) — each type in its own row.
  3. Ask: "Without counting, which row is longest? Shortest? How many more in row A than row B?"
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Concrete graphs make abstract data VISUAL and PHYSICAL. The comparison becomes intuitive — you can SEE it!

Draw a pictograph of the same data. Each object is represented by a small picture or sticker.

  • Label the rows (categories) and the numbers at the bottom or side.
  • Give the graph a TITLE.
💡 A pictograph is like a concrete graph, except pictures replace real objects. It can be read from left to right OR bottom to top.

Show child a prepared pictograph (draw one yourself).

  • Ask: "Which category has the most?" "The fewest?" "How many more does A have than B?"
  • Ask: "What would this look like if one more person chose C?"
📖

The Lesson

A graph turns data (numbers and information) into a PICTURE that is easier to understand at a glance.

📌 Parts of a Pictograph:
• Title — what the graph is about
• Categories — the different groups being compared
• Pictures — each one represents one item (or a set amount)
• Labels — to identify each category
Build a Concrete Graph

Survey your family: "What is your favourite fruit?" (apple, banana, orange, or other)

  1. Collect one object per person (a coin, a block) — put each in the right column.
  2. Read the graph: Which fruit is most popular?
  3. Draw the same data as a pictograph.
Read This Graph

Your parent will draw a pictograph with 4 categories. You answer:

  • Which category has the most?
  • Which has the fewest?
  • How many more does category A have than category C?
  • What is the total for all categories combined?

Likely or Not? — Understanding Probability

D2.1
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

☁️ The weather forecast says there is a "good chance of rain" tomorrow.

Should you bring an umbrella? What does "a good chance" actually MEAN? Is it 50%? 80%? And how does the forecaster even know?

The Big Question: How do we talk about things that MIGHT happen without knowing for sure?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Use probability vocabulary: impossible, unlikely, equally likely, likely, certain.
  • Identify events as more or less likely based on context.
  • Order events from least likely to most likely.
  • Explain probability reasoning using examples.

D2.1

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Draw a line from "Impossible" to "Certain" with "Equally Likely" in the middle.

  • Impossible: rolling a 7 on a regular die
  • Unlikely: rolling a 1 on a die
  • Equally Likely: flipping heads or tails
  • Likely: rolling a number greater than 1 on a die (5 out of 6 sides)
  • Certain: the sun rising tomorrow
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Probability language is intuitive but imprecise at this level — that's fine! The goal is building vocabulary and reasoning, not calculating exact probabilities.

Flip a coin 20 times. Record results. Ask:

  • "Before flipping: was heads likely, unlikely, or equally likely as tails?"
  • "After 20 flips: did we get exactly 10 heads and 10 tails? Why or why not?"
🤯 The coin doesn't 'know' what happened before. Every flip is equally likely to be heads or tails — regardless of what came before!

Roll a die. Ask:

  • "Is rolling a 6 certain, likely, or unlikely?" (unlikely — 1 in 6)
  • "Is rolling an even number likely or unlikely?" (equally likely — 3 in 6)
  • "Is rolling a number less than 7 certain?" (yes — all numbers are 1–6)
📖

The Lesson

Probability describes HOW LIKELY an event is to happen. We can describe probability using special words.

📌 Probability Scale (from least to most likely):
Impossible → Unlikely → Equally Likely → Likely → Certain

  • A dog will learn to fly. (___)
  • Your family will sleep tonight. (___)

  • If you add 2 more blue marbles, does that change the likelihood?

Fair Games — Experimenting with Chance

D2.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

🎯 Two friends play a game: Roll a die. If even (2,4,6), Player A scores a point. If odd (1,3,5), Player B scores a point. Is this game FAIR?

Now: If you roll a number greater than 4 (5 or 6), Player A wins. Otherwise, Player B wins. Is THAT fair?

The Big Question: How do we use math to decide if a game gives everyone an equal chance?

🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Understand what it means for a game or event to be fair or unfair.
  • Connect fairness to equally likely outcomes.
  • Conduct a simple experiment and record results.
  • Compare experimental results with predictions.

D2.2

👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Play two simple games:

  • Game 1: Flip a coin. Heads = you win, Tails = I win. (Fair!)
  • Game 2: Roll a die. 1 or 2 = you win. 3,4,5,6 = I win. (Unfair — I have 4 chances, you have 2.)
👨‍🏫 Teacher Tip: Let child play both games 10 times each. Ask: 'Did the fair game FEEL more equal? Did the unfair game feel unfair?' Connect feelings to mathematical reason.

Design and run a fair game experiment:

  1. Predict: how many times will each outcome happen in 20 trials?
  2. Run the experiment: 20 coin flips or 20 die rolls.
  3. Record: tally marks for each outcome.
  4. Compare: prediction vs actual. Were they the same?
🤯 Experimental results don't always match predictions exactly. With MORE trials, they get closer! This is called the Law of Large Numbers.
📖

The Lesson

A fair game gives every player an equally likely chance of winning. An unfair game gives some players better odds.

📌 To check if a game is fair: count the WINNING outcomes for each player. If both players have the same number of winning outcomes out of all possibilities — the game is fair!
Fair Game Design

Design 2 different games:

  1. A FAIR game using a die. Explain why it is fair.
  2. An UNFAIR game using a die (but don't tell your opponent it's unfair!). How unfair can you make it while still looking fair?
Experiment Journal

Run this experiment: Put 5 red cubes and 5 blue cubes in a bag. Draw one without looking, record it, put it back. Repeat 20 times.

  1. Prediction: ___ times red, ___ times blue
  2. Result: ___ times red, ___ times blue
  3. Conclusion: Was this a fair draw? Why?
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

  1. Attempt: Roll a die. P wins if they roll 1, 2, or 3. Q wins if they roll 4, 5, or 6. Is this fair? Prove it mathematically.
  2. Adjust: Change the game so P rolls a 1–4 and Q rolls a 5–6. Is it still fair? Who has the advantage?
  3. Explore: You predict tails 10 times in a row. Can that happen? Is it likely, unlikely, or impossible?
  4. Design: Create a 3-player fair game using a 6-sided die. How many outcomes does each player get?

📚 Vocabulary Panel

Key words for this strand — read, discuss, and use them!
Data
Information collected about the world, often in numbers or categories.
e.g. The number of sunny days each month is data.
Survey
A method of collecting data by asking people questions.
e.g. 'What is your favourite colour?' is a survey question.
Tally Marks
Quick marks used to count (every 5th mark crosses the other 4).
e.g. |||| = 5 items.
Attribute
A characteristic used to sort or describe objects.
e.g. Colour and size are attributes of a block.
Sort
To organise objects into groups based on a shared attribute.
e.g. Sort buttons by size: small, medium, large.
Pictograph
A graph using pictures to represent data quantities.
e.g. Each 🍎 represents 2 apples eaten.
Bar Graph
A graph using rectangular bars to compare quantities.
e.g. A bar graph of favourite sports compares their popularity.
Category
A group in a graph or sort with a common label.
e.g. 'Blue' is a category in a colour bar graph.
Most / Fewest
The category with the highest / lowest count in a graph.
e.g. 'Red' had the most votes in the survey.
Probability
How likely an event is to happen.
e.g. There is a high probability it will snow in Canada in January.
Certain
An event that will definitely happen.
e.g. The sun rising tomorrow is certain.
Impossible
An event that cannot happen.
e.g. Rolling a 7 on a regular die is impossible.
Likely
An event that will probably happen.
e.g. If a bag has 9 red and 1 blue marble, drawing red is likely.
Unlikely
An event that probably will not happen.
e.g. Drawing the blue marble from that same bag is unlikely.
Equally Likely
Two events that have the same chance of happening.
e.g. Flipping heads or tails is equally likely.
Fair Game
A game where all players have an equal chance of winning.
e.g. A coin-flip game is fair — each player has a ½ chance.

2D Shapes — The Flat World of Geometry

E1.1, E1.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

Close your eyes. Reach into a bag and feel the shape inside — without looking, can you name it just by touching? Shapes have secret fingerprints you can feel with your fingers!
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Identify and name circles, triangles, squares, and rectangles (E1.1)
  • Describe 2D shapes using the words sides and vertices (corners) (E1.1)
  • Sort 2D shapes by their properties (E1.2)
  • Recognize 2D shapes in the environment (E1.1)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Begin with a feely bag: put cut-out shapes (felt or card) in a pillowcase. Your child reaches in, feels one shape, and guesses its name before pulling it out. This activates spatial reasoning — the intuitive geometry knowledge every child has before formal instruction.

After identifying each shape, count sides and vertices together while touching each one. Establish the language firmly: "This is a triangle. It has 3 sides and 3 vertices."

Key shapes and their properties to teach:

  • Circle — 0 sides, 0 vertices, perfectly round, rolls
  • Triangle — 3 sides, 3 vertices (can look different!)
  • Square — 4 equal sides, 4 vertices
  • Rectangle — 4 sides (opposite equal), 4 vertices

Important misconception to address: children often think a tilted square "isn't a square anymore." Rotate cut-outs and confirm: the name doesn't change because you turned it.

Go on a Shape Hunt through your home. Find at least 3 real-world examples of each shape (a clock face = circle; a door = rectangle; a sandwich cut diagonally = triangle; etc.). List them together.

Then sort a mixed pile of cut-out shapes in two different ways: first by number of sides, then by number of vertices. Discuss: "Are there different ways to sort the same shapes?"

Challenge extension: Can your child find a shape that doesn't fit any of the four names? (e.g., a pentagon). Let curiosity open the door to "there are more shapes to learn!"

📖

The Lesson

Look at the world around you — it's full of shapes! A window is a rectangle. A pizza slice is a triangle. The wheel of your bike is a circle. Mathematicians see shapes everywhere because shapes have rules — and rules help us describe the world precisely.

Every 2D shape lives on a flat surface. "2D" means two dimensions: length and width — but no thickness. You could draw every 2D shape on paper.

3D Objects — Shapes That Take Up Space

E1.3, E1.4
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

Can a ball and a box both be 'shapes'? They look SO different — one rolls and one stacks — but mathematicians call them both 3D objects. What could they possibly have in common?
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Identify and name common 3D objects: cube, sphere, cylinder, cone, rectangular prism (E1.3)
  • Describe 3D objects using faces, edges, and vertices (E1.3)
  • Sort 3D objects by their properties (E1.4)
  • Identify 2D shapes on the faces of 3D objects (E3.3)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Gather a collection of 3D objects from around your home:

  • Cube — a dice, a block, a Rubik's cube
  • Sphere — a ball, an orange, a marble
  • Cylinder — a soup can, a toilet paper roll, a cup
  • Cone — an ice cream cone, a party hat, a traffic cone
  • Rectangular prism — a cereal box, a book, a brick

Handle each one. Teach the vocabulary: face (flat surface), edge (where two faces meet), vertex (where edges meet at a point). Count them together.

Key concept: a sphere has no faces, edges, or vertices — it's completely curved. A cylinder has 2 circular faces and 1 curved surface (not a flat face).

Test each 3D object: Can it roll? Can it stack? Can it slide? Create a simple 3-column chart and classify each object. This builds understanding of why shape properties matter in real life — engineers must know whether objects will roll or stack!

Then press each flat face of a 3D object into playdough or trace around each face on paper to reveal its 2D shape. Ask: "What 2D shape is hiding on the face of this cube? This cylinder?"

This makes the critical connection between 2D and 3D geometry explicit.

📖

The Lesson

2D shapes are flat — but the real world is 3D! Everything around you takes up space: it has length, width, and height. Mathematicians call these 3D objects or solids.

Three special words help us describe 3D objects precisely:

  • Face — a flat surface (like the cover of a book)
  • Edge — where two faces meet (like the corner edge of a table)
  • Vertex — where edges meet at a sharp point (like the corner of a box)

E2.1, E2.2
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

A giraffe's neck and a pencil are both 'long.' A key and a city block are both 'short.' Those words don't tell us much! How do mathematicians compare lengths when they don't have a ruler? They invented something clever...
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Measure length and height using non-standard units (E2.1)
  • Compare and order 3 or more objects by length or height (E2.2)
  • Explain that measurement results depend on the unit chosen (E3.2)
  • Record and communicate measurement results (E2.1)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Start with a compelling problem: "I want to tell my friend (who lives in another city) how wide our front door is. I can't send the door. What can I do?" Accept all ideas, then guide toward: measure with something, and describe how many of those somethings fit.

The non-standard measurement approach uses everyday objects as measuring tools:

  • Paper clips laid end-to-end
  • Footsteps (heel-to-toe)
  • Hand spans
  • Pencils
  • Linking cubes

Critical rule to teach: units must be identical, lined up with no gaps or overlaps, starting at one end of the object. If you leave gaps, your measurement is wrong.

This is the heart of non-standard measurement: measure the same object with two different units and get different numbers. Measure the kitchen counter with paper clips AND with hand spans. The counter hasn't changed — but the numbers are different!

Ask: "Which measurement is 'right'?" Both are right — but they used different units, so they got different numbers. This is WHY we eventually invented standard units (centimetres, metres) — so everyone could compare.

Have your child order 5 objects from shortest to longest using paper clips as the unit. Record all measurements in a list or table.

📖

The Lesson

Measuring is one of the oldest human activities. Ancient Egyptians measured using their hands and forearms — called a "cubit." The problem? Everyone's arm is a different length! That's why mathematicians eventually invented standard measuring units that are the same for everyone.

In Grade 1, we start with non-standard units — which means using objects like paper clips or cubes instead of rulers. This helps us understand why standard units were invented!

Measurement Station

  • Your math book: ______ paper clips long
  • A pencil: ______ paper clips long
  • Your hand span (thumb tip to pinky tip): ______ paper clips
  • Your shoe: ______ paper clips long

Now order all 4 objects from shortest to longest: ______, ______, ______, ______

Now measure the same 4 objects with linking cubes. Write the results and compare. Did the order change? Did the numbers change?

💡 When you use a smaller unit (like paper clips), you need MORE of them to measure the same object. When you use a bigger unit (like your hand), you need FEWER. Bigger unit = smaller number. Smaller unit = bigger number. This relationship is one of the big ideas in measurement!

Measuring Mass and Capacity

E2.3, E2.4
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

Which is heavier — a kilogram of feathers or a kilogram of rocks? (Think carefully before you answer!) And can you tell by looking? What if looks are deceiving...
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Compare and order objects by mass using a balance scale (E2.3)
  • Measure mass using non-standard units (E2.3)
  • Compare and order containers by capacity through filling (E2.4)
  • Use the language of mass and capacity: heavier, lighter, holds more, holds less (E2.3, E2.4)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Mass is how much matter is in an object — informally, "how heavy" something is. We cannot reliably judge mass just by looking — the famous riddle proves it (feathers and rocks of the same mass weigh the same, even though feathers look lighter per item).

Make a simple balance scale: hang two identical cups or bags from a coat hanger or pencil. Place objects in each cup and observe which side goes down (heavier) and which rises (lighter).

Now measure mass with non-standard units: use pennies, linking cubes, or small blocks. How many pennies balance this book? How many pennies balance this toy?

Order 5 objects from lightest to heaviest using the balance scale. Use math vocabulary: heavier than, lighter than, the same mass as.

Capacity is the amount a container can hold. Compare capacity by filling containers with water or sand/rice and pouring from one to another.

Gather 4–5 different containers (cups, jars, pots, bottles). Ask: "Which holds the most? Which holds the least?" Let your child guess before comparing.

Compare by filling: fill container A with water, then pour into container B. Does the water overflow? Then A holds more than B. Does it not fill B? Then B holds more than A.

Important: size can be deceiving — a tall, thin glass can hold less than a short, wide bowl. Guide your child to test rather than assume.

📖

The Lesson

Measuring comes in many flavours. Length tells us how far. Mass tells us how heavy. Capacity tells us how much fits inside. Each one needs its own measuring strategy!

Use a balance scale (or make one from a coat hanger and two cups). For each pair of objects, predict which is heavier, then test:

  • Apple vs. orange: I predict ______ is heavier. Result: ______
  • Book vs. pencil case: I predict ______ is heavier. Result: ______
  • Toy car vs. eraser: I predict ______ is heavier. Result: ______

How many pennies do you need to balance your math book? ______ pennies

How many pennies do you need to balance a cup? ______ pennies

Which is heavier — the book or the cup? How many pennies heavier?

Capacity Comparison

Gather 4 containers of different shapes and sizes. Number them 1–4. Fill each with rice or water to find their capacity.

Order them from holds least to holds most:

Least → ______ → ______ → ______ → Most

Was the biggest-looking container the one that held the most? Were you surprised by any result?

💡 Our eyes can fool us about both mass and capacity! A large fluffy stuffed animal might be lighter than a small solid rock. A tall thin vase might hold less water than a short wide bowl. Mathematicians test — they don't just assume.
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

Here is a tricky question: If Container A fills Container B exactly to the top, and Container B fills Container C exactly to the top — which container has the greatest capacity? Which has the least?

💡 Work it out by drawing. If A fills B, then A and B have the same capacity. If B fills C, then B and C have the same capacity. So A = B = C! They all hold the same amount, even if they look very different. This is called 'conservation of capacity' — a big idea in mathematics!

Telling Time — Reading the Clock

E2.5
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

Before clocks existed, how did people know what time it was? They watched the sun! But the sun moves at the same speed every day — and that gave mathematicians an idea for the most beautiful circle-counting machine ever invented.
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Read and show time to the hour on analog and digital clocks (E2.5)
  • Read and show time to the half-hour on analog and digital clocks (E2.5)
  • Use the language of time: o'clock, half past, a.m., p.m. (E2.5)
  • Connect times to daily routines and activities (E2.5)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Make or use a clock with movable hands (a paper plate clock works perfectly). Teach these parts:

  • Hour hand — the short hand; points to the hour
  • Minute hand — the long hand; sweeps around as minutes pass
  • 12 numbers arranged in a circle; 12 at the top, 6 at the bottom

Time to the hour: When the minute hand points straight UP to 12, we read the hour from the short hand. If the short hand points to 3 and the minute hand points to 12: it is 3 o'clock (digital: 3:00).

Practice reading 6 different "o'clock" times. Move the minute hand together: "Now it goes past 12... past 1... past 2..."

Half past: The minute hand has travelled halfway around the clock (from 12 to 6). If the short hand is halfway between 2 and 3, and the minute hand points to 6: it is half past 2 (digital: 2:30).

Walk through your child's full day: "You wake up at 7 o'clock. Breakfast is at 7:30. Math starts at 9 o'clock..." Set the clock to each time. Draw each clock in a daily timeline.

Common confusion: Children often misread which hand is which. Remind them: the SHORT hand is like a short person — they can only reach the hour! The LONG hand can reach all the way to the minutes.

Introduce a.m. (before noon, morning) and p.m. (after noon, afternoon/evening).

📖

The Lesson

An analog clock is a circle divided into 12 equal sections, with two hands that spin around — and every position of those hands means something specific. Reading a clock is like reading a code — once you know the code, you can always know what time it is!

The key to reading time is knowing which hand tells which information:

  • 🔴 Short hand = hour (where we are in the day)
  • 🔵 Long hand = minutes (where we are in the hour)

  • Short hand → halfway between 7 and 8, Long hand → 6: The time is ______

E2.6
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

Why do we have months? Why are there 7 days in a week and not 10? The answer goes back thousands of years — to astronomers who watched the sky and noticed patterns that repeated, again and again, like a mathematical heartbeat.
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Read and use a calendar to identify days, weeks, and months (E2.6)
  • Identify and order the 12 months of the year (E2.6)
  • Connect months to seasons in Ontario (E2.6)
  • Use ordinal numbers to identify dates (1st, 2nd, 3rd...) (E2.6)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Use a real calendar (monthly view). Walk through its structure:

  • Each row = one week (7 days: Sunday through Saturday)
  • Each column = same day of the week, repeating
  • Each square = one day, numbered (the date)
  • The whole page = one month
  • 12 pages = one year

Practise finding: "What day is the 15th?" "How many Wednesdays are in this month?" "What is the date of the last Friday?"

Teach ordinal numbers in context: "Today is the seventh" → 7th. Practice saying: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 5th... through at least 15th.

Teach the 12 months in order, grouped by seasons for Ontario:

  • Winter ❄️ — December, January, February
  • Spring 🌱 — March, April, May
  • Summer ☀️ — June, July, August
  • Autumn/Fall 🍂 — September, October, November

Make a personal connection: "In which month is your birthday? What season is that? What month does school start? End?"

Memory tip for months: January starts with 'Jan' (January, February, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November, December) — practise saying them in a rhythm. Clap once per month.

The Lesson

A calendar is a mathematical tool — a grid that organises time. Without calendars, humans couldn't schedule appointments, plan harvests, or know when seasons were coming. The invention of the calendar was one of the most important mathematical achievements in history!

A calendar is organised by patterns:

  • 7 days → 1 week
  • About 4 weeks → 1 month
  • 12 months → 1 year
  • 4 seasons → 1 year

  4. What day of the week is the first day of this month?
  5. What day of the week is the last day of this month?
  6. How many Mondays are in this month?
  7. What is the date of the third Friday?

What season is it right now? What month will the next season start?

💡 Months are not all the same length! January has 31 days, February has 28 (or 29 in a leap year!), April has 30. A trick to remember: count on your knuckles. A knuckle = 31 days; a gap = 30 days (or 28).

Position and Movement — Where Am I? Where Am I Going?

E3.1, E2.8
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

If I said 'go to the blue house,' would you know where to go? What if there were 10 blue houses? Good directions don't just say WHERE something is — they describe its position compared to other things. Mathematicians invented special words for this!
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Use position words to describe location: above, below, beside, between, in front of, behind (E3.1)
  • Give and follow simple movement instructions: forward, backward, turn left, turn right (E2.8)
  • Describe the position of objects relative to other objects (E3.1)
  • Follow a path described in words or symbols (E2.8)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Spatial language — knowing where things are relative to each other — is a foundational mathematical skill and a strong predictor of later math success. Children who can describe and understand position have an advantage in geometry, measurement, and even algebra.

Use a favourite stuffed animal or toy. Place it in different positions relative to a box or chair and ask: "Where is Teddy?" Guide the child to use precise position words:

  • above / below — vertical relationship
  • beside / next to — horizontal relationship
  • between — in the middle of two things
  • in front of / behind — depth relationship
  • inside / outside — containment
  • on top of / underneath — surface relationship

Then reverse: you describe the position, your child moves the toy to match.

Movement instructions describe how to travel from one location to another. This is the foundation of coding, map reading, and navigation.

Create a simple obstacle course in your home. Give your child movement instructions:

  • 3 steps forward
  • Turn right
  • 2 steps forward
  • Turn left

Then have your child give instructions while you follow them (and make deliberate mistakes to see if they can catch your error).

Map it: draw a simple floor plan of a room and trace the path with a pencil. This connects spatial reasoning to mathematical representation — an important abstraction step.

📖

The Lesson

Where is something? This seems like a simple question — but to describe position precisely, you need two things: a reference point (something to compare to) and position language (words that describe the relationship).

Saying "the book is on the table" is precise because "on" describes the relationship to "the table." Saying "the book is over there" is vague because "there" doesn't tell us the relationship to anything specific.

Counting Money — How Much Do I Have?

F1.3, F1.4, F1.5
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

Can you make 25 cents in more than one way? How many different ways are there? This might seem like a money question, but it's really a mathematics puzzle about combination — and the answer might surprise you!
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Count a collection of same-denomination coins to find total value (F1.3)
  • Count a mixed collection of coins to find total value (F1.3)
  • Represent a given amount using different coin combinations (F1.4)
  • Determine if a collection of coins is enough to purchase an item (F1.5)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Start simple: count a pile of all the same coin type.

Strategy: skip counting. Nickels → count by 5s: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25... Dimes → count by 10s: 10, 20, 30... Quarters → count by 25s: 25, 50, 75, 100...

Practice: lay out 6 nickels. Count by 5s: "5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 — that's 30 cents!" Connect to Strand B patterns — counting by 5s and 10s is the same skip-counting pattern they already know.

Then count pennies (by 1s), dimes (by 10s), and quarters (by 25s). Build fluency with each before mixing.

The standard strategy for counting mixed coins: start with the largest coin, count down.

  1. Sort coins by value (largest first)
  2. Count the largest coins first (e.g., quarters)
  3. Continue with next largest (dimes, then nickels, then pennies)
  4. Say the running total aloud with each coin

Example: 1 quarter + 1 dime + 2 nickels + 3 pennies = "25... 35... 40, 45... 46, 47, 48"

Now play store: price some household objects with stickers (5¢, 10¢, 25¢, 50¢, etc.). Have your child count out the correct coins to "purchase" each item.

📖

The Lesson

Money is only useful if you can count it! There are two skills here: finding the total value of coins you have, and choosing which combination of coins makes a certain amount.

The secret strategy is always the same: start with the biggest coin, count down to the smallest.

Needs and Wants — Making Smart Choices

F1.6, F1.7
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

If you could only choose ONE thing to have — food for the week, or a brand new video game — which would you choose? Does that choice seem fair? Why is it even a choice at all? This question sits at the heart of all financial thinking.
🎯

What You Will Learn

  • Distinguish between needs and wants with examples (F1.6)
  • Explain why both needs and wants cost money (F1.6)
  • Understand that money is limited and choices must be made (F1.7)
  • Categorize spending decisions as needs or wants (F1.6)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

The needs vs. wants distinction is fundamental to financial literacy — and it is not always obvious to children (or adults!).

Needs: things essential for survival and basic well-being. Categories to teach:

  • Food and water (to survive)
  • Shelter (a safe place to live)
  • Clothing (appropriate for climate)
  • Healthcare (when sick or injured)
  • Education (in the modern world)

Wants: things that are nice to have but not essential to survival:

  • Toys, games, extra clothes beyond what's needed
  • Candy, eating out, snacks
  • Streaming services, video games, entertainment
  • Decorations, extra furniture

The grey area: Is a phone a need or a want? Is a bicycle? These "grey area" cases are perfect for discussion — they show that the distinction isn't always simple, which is exactly why financial decisions are hard.

Introduce the key idea: money is limited. You can't buy everything — so you must choose. And choosing one thing often means NOT choosing something else.

Role-play scenario: You have $5.00. You could buy: (a) lunch, or (b) a sticker book. Which do you choose? Why? Is this easy or hard?

Guide the discussion toward: if your lunch is already provided, then the sticker book might be fine. But if you need to buy your own lunch, the food is a need and must come first.

Key message: Needs come before wants when money is limited. This is a foundational principle of financial planning for both families and governments.

📖

The Lesson

Everything costs money — food, clothing, shelter, toys, trips, and services like doctors, teachers, and garbage collection. But most people have a limited amount of money. So they must choose what to buy.

To make smart choices, mathematicians (and wise people of all ages) sort things into two categories:

  • 🏠 Needs — essential things; without them, people would be unsafe, sick, or unable to survive
  • 🎮 Wants — desirable things; life might be less fun without them, but people would survive

Parent Teaching Guide

Children naturally understand instant gratification — but saving requires thinking about the future. Help your child connect saving to something they actually want.

Three key reasons people save:

  1. For a goal: Something you want costs more than you have right now, so you save up
  2. For emergencies: Unexpected things happen (a broken bike, a medical bill); savings provide a cushion
  3. For the future: Planning ahead for known expenses (school supplies, a family trip)

If your child gets any allowance, this is a great time to introduce a simple system: a physical "Save" jar and a "Spend" jar. Even dividing $1.00 into 50¢ save and 50¢ spend builds the habit.

Share a personal story about a time you (the parent) saved for something. This makes the concept real and relatable.

A budget is simply a plan for how to use money before you spend it. Even a Grade 1 child can make a simple budget.

Example budget exercise: "You receive $5.00 for your birthday. You want to buy a book ($3.00) and save the rest for a toy you're working toward. Make a plan."

Budget: $5.00 total → $3.00 spend (book) → $2.00 save → $0.00 left over

Key vocabulary: income (money coming in), spending (money going out), savings (money kept for later), balance (what's left).

Let your child make a pretend birthday budget with a made-up amount of money. This is sophisticated thinking for Grade 1 — celebrate the effort.

📖

The Lesson

Every person who uses money makes the same basic choices: spend now, or save for later? Smart financial thinkers make a plan — called a budget — so they can do both!

Think of your money like a pizza:

  • 🍕 Some slices for things you need RIGHT NOW
  • 🍕 Some slices for things you WANT now (but only if you can afford them)
  • 🍕 Some slices to SAVE for later goals

Parent Teaching Guide

Opportunity cost is one of the most important ideas in economics — and it is accessible even to Grade 1 students through concrete examples.

The definition: When you choose one option, the opportunity cost is the NEXT BEST thing you gave up by not choosing it.

Start with a non-money example: "You have one hour of free time. You could read a book OR play outside. You choose to read. What was your opportunity cost?" → Playing outside.

Then move to money: "You have $5.00. You could buy a snack OR a small toy. You buy the snack. Your opportunity cost is the toy — you gave it up when you chose the snack."

Key insight: You can never have BOTH if you only have money for ONE. Every choice has a cost — the thing you didn't choose.

How do we decide between two options? We think about our values — what matters most to us.

For some people, time with friends matters more than a book. For others, the book matters more. Neither is wrong — it reflects what that person values.

This is a wonderful moment for values discussion: "What do YOU value? Why? Do different members of our family have different values?" This connects mathematics to character education and self-knowledge.

Remind your child: the key to making good decisions is to think BEFORE you spend — just like a mathematician thinks before solving a problem. Impulse buying (buying without thinking) often leads to regret.

The Lesson

Whenever you make a choice, you give something up. This is one of the most interesting ideas in all of economics — and it has a special name: opportunity cost.

The opportunity cost is the next best thing you gave up when you made your choice.

Think Like a Mathematician

Your family has $500 to spend this summer. Option A: A week-long camping trip. Option B: New furniture for the living room. Option C: School supplies and new clothes for September.

Which would you choose? What is the opportunity cost of your choice? Does everyone in your family have the same opinion? Why might they disagree?

💡 When people disagree about financial choices, it's usually because they have different values or different needs. A parent might prioritize school supplies (a need); a child might prefer the camping trip (an experience). Neither is wrong — but families must discuss and decide together. This is why communication about money is so important in families and in governments.

Money in Our Community — How Money Flows

F1.10, F1.11
✨

Wonder Hook — What's the Big Question?

When you buy a banana at the grocery store, that banana has been on a journey — from a farm, to a ship, to a truck, to the store, to your home. And money flowed in the opposite direction at every stop. How does money move through a whole community?
🎯

  • Describe how goods and services are exchanged using money (F1.11)
  • Identify community helpers and how they earn money (F1.10)
  • Understand how money circulates through a community (F1.11)
👩‍🏫

Parent Teaching Guide

Money comes from earning — providing a good or service that others value enough to pay for.

Two key concepts:

  • Goods: physical items you can buy — food, clothing, furniture, toys, books
  • Services: things people do for you — haircuts, teaching, driving, fixing things, medical care

Community helpers and how they earn:

  • Teachers → teach students → receive a salary from the school board
  • Grocery store workers → sell food (goods) → earn wages
  • Doctors → provide healthcare (service) → earn fees or salary
  • Farmers → grow food (goods) → sell to stores and earn money
  • Bus drivers → provide transportation (service) → earn wages
  • Firefighters → provide safety services → earn salaries from the city

Encourage your child to name people in their community and think about what they do to earn money.

Money flows in a circle through a community. This is called the circular flow of money — one of the most important concepts in economics.

Simple example to trace together:

  1. A baker bakes bread (makes a good)
  2. You buy the bread for $3 → baker earns $3
  3. Baker uses that $3 to buy vegetables from a farmer
  4. Farmer uses that $3 to get a haircut
  5. The hairdresser uses that $3 to buy bread from... the baker!

The same $3 kept helping people in the community. This is how a community stays economically healthy: money keeps moving.

Draw this circle together on paper — it's a wonderful visual showing that money is not "used up" when you spend it; it just moves to someone else.

📖

The Lesson

A community is like a giant network of helpers — each person contributes something, and money is the way people acknowledge and reward that contribution. When you spend money at a local store, you help that store owner, who then spends money somewhere else, and the circle keeps going!

Money flows through a community in two directions at once:

  • 🛒 Goods and services flow FROM sellers TO buyers
  • 💰 Money flows FROM buyers TO sellers

These two flows always go in opposite directions — like two rivers flowing past each other!

The $5.00 Journey

Trace the journey of $5.00 through your community. Start with you!

You spend $5.00 at ______. That person uses it at ______. They use it at ______. And so on — trace at least 5 stops in the journey of the same $5.00.

💡 When you buy something from a local business, you are helping your whole community. That money pays the owner's salary, which pays their rent, which pays the landlord, who buys groceries, which pays the grocer... One purchase ripples through an entire community!
🧠

Think Like a Mathematician

What would happen if everyone in your community stopped spending money for one month? What do you think would happen to the businesses? To the workers? To the community?

💡 This is exactly what happened during some difficult periods in history — when people stopped spending, businesses closed, workers lost jobs, and the whole community suffered. This is why economists say 'spending is the engine of the economy.' When money moves, communities thrive. When it stops moving, communities struggle. This is why understanding money matters for everyone, not just for adults.

📚 Vocabulary Panel

Key words for this strand — read, discuss, and use them!
penny
A Canadian coin worth 1 cent (¢). Copper-coloured.
e.g. 1 penny = 1¢.
nickel
A Canadian coin worth 5 cents (¢). Silver-coloured, larger than a dime.
e.g. 2 nickels = 10¢.
dime
A Canadian coin worth 10 cents (¢). Silver-coloured, smallest coin.
e.g. 3 dimes = 30¢.
quarter
A Canadian coin worth 25 cents (¢). Silver-coloured, largest common coin.
e.g. 4 quarters = $1.00.
loonie
A Canadian coin worth 1 dollar ($1). Gold-coloured with a loon.
e.g. 2 loonies = $2.00.
toonie
A Canadian coin worth 2 dollars ($2). Has a gold centre and silver ring.
e.g. 1 toonie = 200¢.
cent
The smallest unit of Canadian money. Symbol: ¢
e.g. 100¢ = $1.00.
dollar
The main unit of Canadian money. Symbol: $. 1 dollar = 100 cents.
e.g. $2.50 = 250¢.
price
How much money something costs.
e.g. The price of the apple is 75¢.
cost
The amount of money needed to buy something.
e.g. The book costs $4.00.
change
The money you get back when you pay more than the price.
e.g. Pay $1.00 for a 75¢ item → get 25¢ change.
need
Something essential that people must have to survive and be safe.
e.g. Food, shelter, and clothing are needs.
want
Something you would like to have but could survive without.
e.g. A new toy or a video game is a want.
save
To keep money instead of spending it — for a future goal.
e.g. Saving 50¢ a week to buy a book.
spend
To use money to buy goods or services.
e.g. Spending $2.00 on a snack.
earn
To receive money in exchange for work or a service.
e.g. A baker earns money by selling bread.
budget
A plan for how to use your money.
e.g. Spending plan: $5 save, $3 spend, $2 give.
goods
Physical items that can be bought and sold.
e.g. Bread, shoes, and toys are goods.
services
Things people do for others in exchange for money.
e.g. Teaching, haircuts, and driving are services.
opportunity cost
The next best thing you gave up when you made a choice.
e.g. Choosing candy means giving up saving for a toy.
