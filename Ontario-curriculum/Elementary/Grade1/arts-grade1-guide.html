📖The Lesson — What Is Dance?

Your Body Has Always Been Talking

Before humans invented writing, before they had alphabets, before they built cities — they danced. The oldest paintings we have ever found, made on cave walls over 30,000 years ago, show people moving together. Dance is one of the oldest human activities on Earth.

Why? Because your body is incredibly good at expressing things that words cannot always reach. When you are so happy you cannot stand still, your body knows what to do — it jumps, it spins, it bounces. When you are angry, you might stomp. When you are sad, your whole body droops. Your body is already dancing, every single day. Dance class just teaches you to do it on purpose and with skill.

Scientists who study babies have discovered that human infants — even babies just a few months old — bob and sway when they hear music. No one teaches them to do this. It appears to be completely natural. Rhythm and movement may be built into us.

Dance as Language

Think of language as a code — a system for sharing what is in your mind with another person. English is a language. French is a language. Math symbols are a language. And movement is a language too.

When a dancer reaches their arms upward with palms open, most people in the world will feel something — perhaps freedom, or joy, or prayer. When a dancer curls inward, arms wrapped around themselves, most people will feel something like sadness or protection. This is because your brain is wired to read bodies. It happens automatically, in less than a second. This is called kinesthetic empathy — feeling in your own body what you see in someone else's.

Dance Is Found Everywhere

Every single human culture ever studied by scientists has dance. Every one. This makes dance universal — meaning it belongs to all humans, not just one group.

The Haka of the Māori people of New Zealand is a powerful, stomping dance performed before battles and important events. Its sharp movements and fierce expressions convey strength and unity.

The Bharatanatyam of South India uses over 64 hand gestures called mudras, each with a specific meaning — creating an entire vocabulary of shapes.

The powwow dances of many Indigenous peoples of North America, including peoples right here in Ontario, use circular movement and regalia (special clothing) to tell stories and honour ancestors.

The waltz of Europe, the samba of Brazil, the breakdancing that started in the Bronx in New York in the 1970s — all of these are different forms of the same human need: to express something through movement.

A dancer named Isadora Duncan (1877–1927) changed the history of dance forever. At a time when ballet was the "proper" way to dance, she kicked off her tight shoes, put on a loose dress, and danced the way she felt — bare feet on the ground, inspired by ancient Greek sculpture. People were shocked. Then they were amazed. She proved that movement itself, without fancy footwork or tutus, could be powerful art.

What Makes Something Dance?

Here is a question that even professional dancers argue about: what is the difference between walking and dancing?

Both use your legs. Both move you through space. What is different? The answer that most dance teachers and scholars agree on is this: dance is movement that is purposeful and expressive. It is movement that means something — either to the person doing it or to the people watching, or both. A person walking quickly to catch a bus is not dancing. A person walking slowly with enormous deliberate weight and sadness, choosing every step — that could be dance.

This is good news for you as a beginning dancer: you do not need to know any specific steps or techniques to make dance. You already have the most important ingredient: a body that can move, and something inside you that wants to express itself.

📖The Lesson — The Dancer's Toolkit

Dancers Have a Vocabulary

Imagine you want to build a house. You need materials: wood, bricks, glass, concrete. Each material does something different. Dancers also have "materials" they use to build a dance. These are called the elements of dance, and there are five of them: Body, Space, Time, Energy, and Relationships. This lesson explores the first two.

Element 1: BODY — What Are You Doing?

The Body element is about which parts of your body are moving and how they are moving.

Body Parts: You can dance with your whole body, or you can isolate — that means move just ONE part while everything else stays still. Try it: keep everything perfectly still and move only your right shoulder. Now just your eyebrows. Now just your knees. Isolation is a technique used in many dance styles from hip hop to ballet.

Body Shapes: Your body makes shapes in space. Shapes can be:

  • Wide: arms stretched out, taking up maximum space — like a starfish
  • Narrow: everything pulled in tight, like a pencil
  • Curved: the body makes rounded arcs and bends
  • Twisted: different parts of the body face different directions at once
  • Angular: the body makes sharp zig-zag shapes with elbows, knees, and hips
  • Symmetrical: both sides of the body match each other
  • Asymmetrical: each side does something different

Body Actions: Actions are the verbs of dance. The main body actions are: travel (moving through space), turn (rotating), jump (leaving the floor), gesture (moving a body part without travelling), stillness (stopping — yes, stillness is an action!), balance (holding a position), and transfer of weight (shifting your weight from one part of your body to another).

Stillness in dance is often more powerful than movement. When a dancer stops completely in the middle of a piece, the audience's attention locks onto them. Choreographers call this a "freeze" or a "held moment." Silence in music works the same way.

Element 2: SPACE — Where Are You Moving?

Space is all around you, and dancers think about it very carefully. There are several dimensions of space:

Level: How high or low you are in the space.

  • Low level: close to the floor — crawling, rolling, lying
  • Medium level: in the middle — crouching, sitting, walking normally
  • High level: reaching up, on tiptoes, jumping

Changing levels is one of the most powerful tools a dancer has. Moving from high to low can represent a fall, submission, or exhaustion. Moving from low to high can represent rising, hope, or growing strength.

Direction: Where you are moving to. Forward, backward, sideways, diagonal, up, down — and every combination in between.

Size: How much space your movement takes up. Large or expansive movements fill the space and feel bold, powerful, or free. Small or contained movements feel intimate, delicate, secret, or frightened.

Pathway: The pattern your movement traces on the floor (or in the air). Pathways can be straight, curved, zigzag, circular, spiral, or random. A curved pathway feels gentle and flowing; a sharp zigzag pathway feels agitated or chaotic.

Personal Space vs. General Space: Your personal space is the bubble of space immediately around your body — the space you can reach without moving from where you stand. General space is all the space in the room that you and other dancers share.

The famous choreographer Martha Graham (1894–1991) believed that a dancer's spine was the centre of all expression. She built an entire dance technique around the spine — particularly the movement of "contraction" (curling the spine inward) and "release" (opening it outward). She believed the contraction represented the deepest human emotions. Her technique is still taught in dance schools around the world.

Putting Body and Space Together

These two elements always work together. A wide body shape (Body element) at a high level (Space element) moving in a circular pathway (Space element) creates a very different feeling than a narrow body shape at a low level moving in a sharp zigzag pathway. Try both and feel the difference. Notice how your emotional experience of the movement completely changes.

📖The Lesson — Time and Energy

Element 3: TIME — When and How Fast?

Every movement happens in time. Time has several important dimensions in dance:

Speed (Tempo): How fast or slow a movement is. Tempo comes from the Italian word for "time." A movement can be very fast (like a hummingbird's wing), very slow (like a glacier moving), or anywhere in between.

Rhythm: A pattern of beats, like a heartbeat or a drum. Rhythm gives movement structure. Your heart beats in a rhythm: ba-DUM, ba-DUM, ba-DUM. Some dances follow a steady, predictable rhythm. Others deliberately break the rhythm — and the surprise of that break is what creates excitement or tension.

Beat: The steady, even pulse of time. Like the ticking of a clock. A dancer can move ON the beat (moving exactly with each tick), AGAINST the beat (moving in between the ticks), or with accents (emphasizing certain beats more than others).

Duration: How long a movement lasts. A very long, sustained movement is called sustained. A very short, sharp movement is called percussive. Most dances use a mixture of both.

The conductor of an orchestra waves a baton in rhythmic patterns to keep all musicians playing at the same time. Dance can serve the same function: early human dances were often used to synchronize groups — helping hunters move together, warriors fight together, farmers harvest together. Synchronized movement builds community.

Element 4: ENERGY — How Does It Feel?

Energy in dance is not about how tired or alert you are — it is about the quality or texture of your movement. Two dancers can make identical shapes in identical time, but if their energy is different, the movement feels completely different to the audience.

Rudolf Laban, a Hungarian choreographer who lived from 1879 to 1958, created a system for describing movement energy. He identified what he called effort qualities:

Weight:

  • Strong/Heavy: Movement that has weight behind it — like pushing a heavy door, or a thunderclap. The body commits fully to the movement.
  • Light/Fine: Movement that is barely there — like touching a soap bubble, or a feather landing. Very little muscular force is used.

Flow:

  • Free Flow: Movement that once started feels hard to stop — it carries you along, like water going downhill, or a spinning top. This feels uncontrolled, abandoned, released.
  • Bound Flow: Movement that is controlled at every moment — you could stop at any point, like a robot that checks each gear before engaging the next. This feels careful, tense, controlled.

Time (as an energy quality):

  • Sudden/Quick: Movement that happens abruptly, with urgency — like a spark or a flinch.
  • Sustained/Slow: Movement that unfolds gradually and deliberately — like a sunrise, or melting chocolate.

Space (as an energy quality):

  • Direct: Movement that goes straight to its destination, no wandering, no detours — like an arrow.
  • Indirect/Flexible: Movement that meanders, curves, explores — like a butterfly.
Hip hop dance is a fascinating study in energy contrasts. In a single freestyle, a dancer might alternate between explosive, percussive isolations (very sudden, strong energy) and smooth, flowing glides (slow, free, light). This contrast — between "hitting" and "gliding" — is one of the defining characteristics of the art form. Pioneers like Richard "Crazy Legs" Colón and Michael Jackson made these contrasts famous.

Contrasts Are the Key to Meaning

The most powerful tool a dancer has is not any single quality — it is the contrast between qualities. A piece that is entirely slow becomes monotonous. A piece that is entirely fast becomes exhausting and loses meaning. But when a dancer moves fast, fast, fast — and then suddenly STOPS, completely still, in complete silence — that stillness has enormous impact because of the contrast.

Think of it like seasoning food: salt by itself is not delicious. But a pinch of salt in a sweet dessert makes the sweetness sing. Contrast in dance is the same — one quality highlights the other.

📖The Lesson — Dance Tells Stories

Can Dance Tell a Story?

Yes — and it has been doing so for thousands of years. The question is: how?

Dance stories are different from book stories or movie stories. A book uses words. A movie uses images and dialogue. Dance uses movement — and that means it works differently in the brain of the audience.

When you watch a dance and feel something — excitement, sadness, unease, joy — you are not "figuring out" the story the way you would read a paragraph. Instead, the kinesthetic response we learned about earlier takes over: your mirror neurons (special brain cells) simulate the dancer's movement in your own body, and you feel what they feel, even though you are sitting still.

Three Ways Dance Tells Stories

1. Mimetic Dance (Mime): The most literal form. The dancer pretends to be doing the actual thing — swimming, cooking, crying. This is fun and readable but considered the least artistically sophisticated because it simply copies real life rather than transforming it.

2. Representative Dance: The dancer represents characters or events through somewhat stylized movement — more exaggerated than mime, using the elements of dance, but still fairly "readable." Many folk dances and storytelling dances work this way.

3. Abstract Dance: The dancer does not pretend to be anyone or anything specific. Instead, they use the elements of dance to create the feeling of the story. An audience watching an abstract dance about grief might feel the weight and sadness without anyone pretending to cry. This is the most challenging and the most powerful form.

The ballet "Swan Lake" (1875) tells a story using representative dance: the dancers are playing characters (a prince, a princess transformed into a swan, an evil sorcerer). But even in ballet, the meaning is not entirely in the story — a famous ballerina's performance of the dying swan scene is so moving not because we're watching someone pretend to die, but because the quality of the movement itself — the weight, the flow, the pace — creates real grief in the audience.

The Three-Part Arc

Almost every story in human history has a structure: a beginning, a middle, and an end. This is called a narrative arc. Dance stories follow this too, but instead of "first this happened, then this, then that," the arc is felt through changes in the dance elements:

  • Beginning: usually establishes a state — who is here, what is the energy, what is the world like? Often uses stable, clear movement.
  • Middle: something changes or grows or breaks — more complex movements, contrasts, tension, instability.
  • End: a resolution — the energy settles, transforms, or completely breaks. The ending of a dance shapes what the audience carries away.

Abstraction: Moving Beyond Mime

Here is an experiment in abstraction. The story is: "A person discovers they can fly."

Mime version: Spread arms like wings. Run. Look surprised. Rise on tiptoes.

Abstract version: Begin low, weight heavy, movement small and bound. Gradually the movement gains free flow — arms not like wings but opening in ways that feel unguarded, dangerous, free. Level rises. The quality shifts from bound/heavy/direct to free/light/indirect. The body seems to be discovering that gravity is optional.

Notice: in the abstract version, you are not pretending to be a person who is flying. You are using the QUALITIES of flight — freedom, rising, the relationship between weight and weightlessness — to create the emotional experience of the idea.

Alvin Ailey (1931–1989) was an African American choreographer who changed modern dance forever. His most famous piece, "Revelations" (1960), tells the story of African American spiritual life through abstract dance set to traditional Black American church music. He didn't need words. The weight of the dancers' arms in the opening sequence alone communicates centuries of endurance. It remains one of the most performed dance pieces in history.

📖The Lesson — The Fifth Element: Relationships

You Are Never Alone in Dance

Even when a dancer performs completely solo — alone on a stage, the only person in the room — they are in relationship. They are in relationship with the space around them, with the floor beneath them, with the music or silence, and with the audience watching.

The fifth element of dance is Relationships. It describes how a dancer relates to others and to the environment.

Dancer-to-Dancer Relationships

When two or more dancers perform together, their relationship is one of the most expressive things the audience watches. Here are the main relationship types:

  • Mirroring: One dancer does something and the other copies it exactly — like looking in a mirror. This creates a feeling of connection, unity, or reflection. It can feel tender, eerie, or playful depending on the energy.
  • Shadowing: One dancer follows another, slightly behind and copying their pathway. This can feel like a memory, a ghost, or a self-doubt following someone.
  • Call-and-Response: One dancer does a phrase, then another "answers" with their own phrase. This is borrowed from African American music traditions (where it's used in gospel, jazz, and blues) and creates a feeling of conversation.
  • Canon: Multiple dancers do the same phrase but at different times — like a musical round. One starts, then a second starts when the first is halfway through, etc. Creates rippling effects and feelings of waves or spreading ideas.
  • Unison: All dancers do exactly the same thing at exactly the same time. Creates power, solidarity, clarity — a shared statement.
  • Contrast: Different dancers do different things simultaneously — one moving slowly while another moves fast, one at high level while another is low. Creates tension, debate, complexity.
Contact improvisation is a form of dance where two or more people move together by sharing physical weight — leaning on each other, counterbalancing, rolling across each other's bodies. It was invented in the 1970s by Steve Paxton, and it has no choreography — everything is improvised in the moment. The relationship IS the dance.

Dancer-to-Space Relationships

A dancer can also be in relationship with the space itself. Moving toward something (approaching) or away from something (retreating) creates meaning. Filling all the space versus clustering together creates meaning. A dancer who consistently stays in one small corner of the stage is in relationship with that corner — the audience will wonder why.

Dancer-to-Object Relationships

Props — objects used in dance — can create fascinating relationships. A piece of cloth carried by a dancer can become a wave, a burden, a lover, a memory. The dancer's relationship with the object tells a story without words.

In many traditional dances around the world, the relationship between dancers is not optional or decorative — it is the whole point. In the Saman dance of the Gayo people of Aceh, Indonesia, dozens of dancers must move in perfect synchrony — their upper bodies weaving in unison at incredible speed. Any error is immediately visible. The unity itself is the message: we are one people, we move as one.

📖The Lesson — How to Make a Dance

The Creative Process in Dance

Making a dance is not a mystery — it is a process. Every choreographer, from beginners to masters, follows some version of the same steps. Learning this process gives you a way to start even when you have no ideas — and to keep going even when you feel stuck.

Step 1: IMAGINE — Finding Your Starting Point

Every dance starts from something. That something can be almost anything: an emotion you want to explore. An image you can't get out of your head. A piece of music. A question you have. Something you noticed in nature. A story you want to tell. A word. A colour. A texture. A memory.

Professional choreographers keep notebooks, journals, sketchbooks, and voice memos to capture starting points whenever they appear. The starting point is not the dance — it is the door into the dance.

Step 2: EXPLORE — Improvise Without Judgment

Improvisation means moving without a plan — letting the body discover what it wants to do in response to the starting point. This is where most of your raw material comes from.

The most important rule of improvisation: do not edit while you create. Your inner critic (the voice that says "that looks stupid" or "that's not good enough") must be silenced during exploration. Every movement that happens during improvisation is interesting data, even the ones that don't work. Especially the ones that don't work.

Useful improvisation prompts: "What if I let this emotion lead my whole body?" "What if I pretended the floor was made of this texture?" "What if I could only move one specific body part and everything else was frozen?" "What if I moved as if I was underwater? In space? In a crowd?"

Step 3: SELECT — Choose What to Keep

After improvising, you look at what you found and decide: Which moments felt most true? Which surprised you? Which felt boring or forced? You keep the interesting ones and set aside the others (not "throw away" — good ideas you don't use now might be right for a different piece later).

Step 4: DEVELOP — Shape and Refine

You take your selected material and begin to shape it. You might repeat a movement you liked and discover a variation. You might try it at a different speed, level, or with different energy. You connect phrases together. You decide what comes first, what comes last, and what the most powerful moment is.

Step 5: PRESENT — Share the Work

At some point, the dance is ready to be shared — even if it doesn't feel "finished." Sharing is part of the creative process, not the end of it. The audience's response becomes information that shapes how you see your own work.

Step 6: REFLECT — Learn from What You Made

After sharing, you ask yourself honestly: What worked? What surprised me? What didn't work the way I intended? What would I do differently? What did the audience see that I didn't see? Reflection is not criticism — it is curiosity about your own creative mind.

Choreographer Twyla Tharp (born 1941) is famous for keeping meticulous notes on every idea she has. She stores these in boxes — years of movement ideas, magazine clippings, music fragments, words, memories — which she calls her "choreographic compost." When she needs to make a new dance, she digs through the compost. She has described her creative process as "making connections between things that don't obviously connect." This is not so different from what you do in this lesson.

📖The Lesson — How to Watch Dance

The Audience Is Not Passive

When you watch a live dance performance, something remarkable happens in your brain. Your mirror neurons — the same brain cells we discussed in Lesson 1 — fire as if you yourself are moving. You feel, slightly, in your own muscles, what the dancer is doing. This is why watching someone fall makes you flinch, and watching someone jump makes your stomach lift.

This means that watching dance is not a passive activity. You are participating. Your attention, your responses, your breathing — all of this affects the performance. A performer can feel a focused, engaged audience versus a distracted one. A great audience raises the quality of a performance. The audience is completing the work of art.

Three Levels of Critical Response

Arts critics — people who write and think professionally about dance — have a structured approach to responding that goes much deeper than "I liked it" or "I didn't." Here are three levels you will practice:

Level 1 — DESCRIBE: What do you actually see? Before interpreting or evaluating, simply describe the movement as if you were explaining it to someone who cannot see: What are the dancers doing? What are their body shapes? Levels? Energies? Is there music? What does the space look like?

Level 2 — INTERPRET: What do you think it means? What story, emotion, or idea does it seem to be about? Connect specific movements to your interpretation: "When the dancer hunches over and moves with small, tight steps, it feels like fear or shame, because..."

Level 3 — EVALUATE: How effectively does the dance achieve its purpose? Not "did you like it?" but: Did the movement choices serve the intention? Were there moments that worked very well? Moments that confused or did not connect? Why?

Professional dance critics often see the same performance multiple times before writing their review. After the first viewing, they write impressions and feelings. After the second, they analyze specific movement choices. After the third, they can evaluate how the whole piece fits together. You don't have to see everything at once — multiple viewings reveal different layers.

Respectful Critical Language

Critical language should be specific, honest, and kind. Here are some phrases you can use:

  • "When the dancer did _____, I felt _____, because..."
  • "The use of _____ [element] in this moment seemed to suggest..."
  • "I found it difficult to understand _____, but I wonder if the choreographer intended..."
  • "The most effective moment for me was _____ because..."
  • "I notice that _____ — and I'm not sure yet what to make of it."

Notice that these phrases are curious, not dismissive. They make space for the possibility that you haven't fully understood something yet — which is always true. Dance is rich enough that you will never understand it fully in one viewing.

📖The Lesson — Dance Builds Community

Dance Is Technology

Not technology like a phone or a computer — but technology in its original sense: a tool that humans invented to solve a problem. What problem does dance solve? The problem of how to be together.

Humans are deeply social animals. We need to feel connected — to each other, to our ancestors, to our beliefs, to our sense of who we are as a group. Dance is one of the most powerful tools humans have ever invented for creating that connection.

Dance as Memory

Before writing was widespread, oral and movement traditions were how knowledge was passed from generation to generation. The steps of a traditional dance carry history in them. When a young person learns a traditional dance from their grandparent, they are not just learning steps — they are receiving a living document of where their people come from, what they believe, and how they understand the world.

This is why the banning of Indigenous dances in Canada (which happened under the Canadian government's potlatch ban from 1885 to 1951) was so devastating. It was not just banning a leisure activity — it was banning living cultural memory. The revitalization of Indigenous dance traditions across Canada today is an act of cultural healing and reclamation.

Dance Traditions in Canada

Indigenous Dance Traditions of Ontario: The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) peoples have complex ceremonial dances that mark the seasons, celebrate life events, and honour the Creator. The Anishinaabeg traditions include the Drum Dance, where singers and drummers lead the community in celebration and prayer. Many First Nations communities also participate in powwow traditions, which are intertribal gatherings that include competitive and social dancing in many specific styles including: Jingle Dress Dance (a healing dance), Fancy Shawl Dance, Men's Traditional Dance, and many more.

African-Canadian Traditions: African-Caribbean communities in Ontario bring traditions including Carnival/Soca dancing, which connects to West African and Caribbean heritage. These traditions of energetic, colourful, communal celebration carry immense joy and cultural pride.

South Asian Traditions: Ontario is home to large communities whose traditions include Bhangra (a high-energy Punjabi folk dance associated with harvest festivals), Bharatanatyam (a classical South Indian temple dance form), and Garba (a Gujarati tradition performed during the Navratri festival).

European Folk Traditions: Many Canadians trace ancestry to countries with rich folk dance traditions: Ukrainian hopak, Greek syrtos, Irish step dancing, Eastern European polkas and waltzes. These are performed at community festivals across Ontario every summer.

Dance and Social Justice

Dance has also been used as a tool of resistance and assertion. Hip hop dance emerged from Black communities in New York who had limited access to formal arts institutions — the street and community centre became the stage. In South Africa during apartheid, township dance was a way to maintain dignity and cultural identity under oppressive laws. In colonized countries around the world, traditional dance was often the thread that held cultural identity together when language, land, and other markers of identity were systematically attacked.

In 1986, a young woman named Carolyn Durocher, an Anishinaabe, began researching and reviving the Jingle Dress Dance after her community had been discouraged from practicing it. Today it is one of the most recognizable and celebrated dance forms at powwows across North America. The jingles (metal cones) on the dress are said to carry healing prayers — the sound itself is medicine.

Dance in Your Community

You don't have to go to a professional performance to find dance in your community. Dance is happening at birthday parties, at places of worship, at cultural festivals, at school events, on social media, in living rooms and backyards. Part of being a dancer — even a young one — is recognizing dance everywhere, and understanding that all of it carries meaning.

Drama may feel unfamiliar or even embarrassing to adults who haven't done it since childhood. Your child will learn best if you are willing to enter the work alongside them — making the classroom activities a shared exploration, not just your child performing for you.

Core idea: Drama is not lying. It is a structured, consensual agreement to "play" in a way that explores human experience safely. 📖The Lesson — Drama: The Art of Living in Story

The Most Ancient Art?

There is a cave in South Africa called Blombos Cave where archaeologists found ochre crayons and decorated shells over 75,000 years old. They believe humans were making art that long ago. But drama — the art of acting out stories, taking on roles, pretending to be others — may be even older than visual art. Children do it naturally before they can read or write. Animals do versions of it (young animals play-fight to practice adult skills). Drama may be woven into what it means to be a human mind.

What is drama? Drama is the art form in which people use their bodies, voices, and imaginations to bring characters and stories to life, usually in front of an audience — though not always. Drama includes theatre (performed in a dedicated space), storytelling, puppetry, improvisation, dramatic play, and ritual.

Elements of Drama

Just as dance has elements (Body, Space, Time, Energy, Relationships), drama has its own set of building blocks:

  • Role/Character: Who you are pretending to be. A role can be a specific named character (Cinderella, a superhero) or a general type (a merchant, a farmer, a queen). Character goes deeper — it's the full inner life of the person: their fears, desires, memories, and beliefs.
  • Narrative/Story: What happens. Every drama has a sequence of events. Even the simplest dramatic game has a story arc: something happens, it gets complicated, and something resolves.
  • Tension/Conflict: The problem or challenge that creates drama. Without conflict, there is no drama — things just happen without stakes. The conflict can be between two characters, between a character and nature, or inside a single character's own mind.
  • Focus: What we are paying attention to. In good drama, the most important thing in the scene is where the audience's focus is directed.
  • Symbol: An object, gesture, or moment that represents something larger than itself. A candle that goes out represents hope failing. A handshake represents agreement or reconciliation.
  • Time and Place (Setting): When and where the story happens. Setting shapes everything — a story set in a dark cave feels completely different from the same story set in a bright kitchen.
  • Space: Where the drama happens and how the space is used.
  • Language and Voice: What is said and how it is said. The same words spoken differently can mean completely different things.
  • Movement and Gesture: How characters' bodies express their inner lives.
The word "person" comes from the Latin "persona," which meant "mask" — the masks worn by actors in ancient Greek and Roman theatre. The idea was that the mask allowed the actor to become someone else. We still talk about our "persona" today — the face we show the world. Drama has been shaping how we understand human identity for over 2,500 years.

Drama vs. Real Life

Here is one of the strangest things about drama: the emotions can be completely real, even though the situation is completely pretend. An actor playing grief often genuinely feels something like grief. An actor playing fear often genuinely feels something like fear. But they do this on purpose, in a controlled way, in service of a story.

Constantin Stanislavski (1863–1938) was a Russian theatre director who developed a system for helping actors access genuine emotions through techniques like "emotional memory" (recalling a personal experience to fuel a scene). His method is still the foundation of most Western acting training today.

Drama in Every Life

You might think drama only happens on stage. But dramatic thinking is everywhere:

  • When you imagine how a conversation might go before having it, you are doing a kind of drama
  • When you try to understand why someone acted the way they did by putting yourself in their position, you are using dramatic empathy
  • When you tell a story and you "do the voices" of different people, you are performing drama
  • When you play a video game that lets you make choices as a character, you are engaging in interactive drama
In many Indigenous cultures of Canada, storytelling ceremonies involve the storyteller not just telling a story but performing it — becoming the animals, the spirits, and the humans in the tale. This is drama in its most ancient form. The Cree concept of âtayohkêwina (sacred stories) uses dramatic performance to transmit deep cultural knowledge and spiritual understanding. These are not entertainment — they are living education.

📖The Lesson — Voice and Body as Performance

The Voice

Your voice is the most flexible instrument ever created. No manufactured instrument can match the range of expressiveness that the human voice carries. Actors spend years training their voices to have complete control over every quality. Here are the main qualities you will learn to control:

Pitch: How high or low your voice is. A very high pitch often conveys excitement, nervousness, or youth. A very low pitch conveys authority, seriousness, or danger. Changing pitch MID-sentence (called inflection) is how we make meaning clear in spoken language — it is the music of speech.

Volume: How loud or quiet you are. Volume is power — and the softest voice in a room is sometimes the most powerful, because people have to be very still and close to hear it. Shouting can feel powerful but also loses effectiveness quickly; a whisper can stop a room.

Pace: How fast or slow you speak. Fast speech feels urgent, excited, or nervous. Slow speech feels deliberate, sorrowful, or threatening. Varying your pace within a speech is one of the marks of a skilled actor.

Pause: Silence within speech. A pause is one of the most powerful tools a speaker has. It creates anticipation, emphasizes what comes after it, and gives the audience time to feel what just happened. Many amateur actors are afraid of pauses — they rush to fill silence. Professional actors understand that the pause IS the meaning.

Articulation: How clearly you form each sound and word. In everyday conversation we often swallow sounds. On stage or in storytelling, clear articulation makes text understandable and gives the voice precision.

Tone: The emotional quality of the voice — warm, cold, ironic, tender, harsh, playful. Tone is what the voice "feels like" to the listener.

Radio actors in the 1930s–1950s had to create entire worlds — including gunfights, spaceship interiors, ocean voyages — using only voice and sound effects. The voice had to do everything visual art could not. Listeners would sit around their radios and genuinely experience fear, excitement, and love from disembodied voices. This remains one of the most remarkable demonstrations of vocal power in media history.

The Body

Before you say a word, your body has already spoken. Research suggests that the vast majority of interpersonal communication is nonverbal — your posture, facial expression, and distance from another person communicate more than your words.

Posture: How you hold your body. Upright, open posture communicates confidence and presence. Hunched, closed posture communicates insecurity, sadness, or fear. Leaning forward shows interest. Leaning back shows either relaxation or withdrawal. Every posture carries meaning.

Gesture: Movement of hands, arms, or head that accompanies speech or stands alone. Gestures can emphasize words, replace words (nodding instead of saying "yes"), or contradict words (saying "I'm fine" while shaking your head). In drama, gestures must be intentional — chosen, not habitual.

Facial Expression: The face is the most expressive surface of the body. Eyes, eyebrows, mouth, and forehead can create thousands of combinations. The key lesson for drama: in everyday life, we often suppress facial expression. In performance, you must let the face do what it wants to do — and sometimes amplify it.

Proxemics: The study of physical distance between people. In English-speaking cultures, there are roughly four distance zones: intimate (0–45cm — reserved for loved ones), personal (45cm–1.2m — friends and family), social (1.2m–3.6m — acquaintances), and public (3.6m+ — formal situations). Drama deliberately plays with these zones. Moving into someone's intimate space uninvited creates threat or intensity. Maintaining public distance from a close friend creates emotional coldness. Choreographing proxemics is a powerful directorial tool.

Deaf theatre has pushed our understanding of what drama can be. Companies like the National Theatre of the Deaf (founded 1967) create performances that are simultaneously in sign language and spoken English — and critics consistently describe them as more visually expressive than standard theatre. When the body carries the full burden of language, the expressiveness of gesture and posture becomes extraordinary.

Role Play and Empathy — Stepping Into Someone Else

Curriculum: DR1.2 · DR2.1
🌟Wonder Hook

Here is a true story: in 1987, a teacher named Jane Elliott divided her Grade 3 class in half. Blue-eyed students were told they were smarter and better. Brown-eyed students were given collars to wear and told they were inferior. Within hours, the blue-eyed students were performing better on tests. The brown-eyed students — who had been succeeding before — began to fail. Friendships broke apart. Children who had been gentle became cruel.

Then she switched it. The blue-eyed students got collars. The brown-eyed students became "superior." The same thing happened in reverse.

What does this experiment teach us about how we understand our own identity? And how could drama — which asks you to step into another person's experience — be related to this experiment?

📖The Lesson — Walking in Another's Shoes

What Is Dramatic Empathy?

Empathy is the ability to understand and share the feelings of another person. It is one of the most important human capacities — without it, cooperation, kindness, and justice become nearly impossible.

Dramatic empathy takes this a step further. Instead of just imagining how someone else feels, you physically enter their perspective — you speak as them, move as them, make decisions as them. You are not observing their experience; you are inhabiting it.

Research in neuroscience suggests that acting out another person's experience — even briefly, even in play — can genuinely shift your understanding of their perspective. It is different from reading about someone (which activates the verbal/language parts of the brain) because it also activates the sensory and motor parts. Your whole brain is engaged when you physically embody another perspective.

The Drama of Everyday Life

Role play is something you can do with any situation — not just scripted plays. You can role play:

  • A difficult conversation you need to have
  • A historical event, from multiple perspectives
  • A decision where you need to understand all sides
  • A character from a book, to understand their choices
  • A job or responsibility you haven't had yet, to prepare for it
  • A conflict in your own life, played from the other person's point of view

Multiple Perspectives

The most powerful use of role play is playing the SAME situation from multiple different perspectives. Consider this scenario: a factory is being built in a small town. The factory will bring jobs but will create pollution in the local river.

Now imagine playing: a parent who needs work to feed their family / a child who swims in the river / the factory owner / a scientist who studies the river ecosystem / an Elder from a local Indigenous community whose ancestors have used the river for centuries / a politician whose job depends on bringing industry to the town.

Every one of these people is not wrong — they each have legitimate concerns and real stakes. There is no simple answer. This is the power of drama: it holds multiple truths at once without forcing a single conclusion.

Dorothea Heathcote (1926–2011) was a British drama educator who developed "Mantle of the Expert" — a drama technique where children are given the roles of experts (archaeologists, scientists, rescue workers, historians) and then put in situations where they must use their expert knowledge dramatically. Children who play archaeologists and discover a fictional ancient site often later score much higher on history tests than those who were simply taught the facts. The embodied experience of being in role creates learning that lecture cannot.

The Double Awareness

When you are doing drama, you are always two people at once: yourself AND the character. This is called double awareness. If you lose one of these — if you completely forget you are yourself and believe you have genuinely become the character — this can be psychologically overwhelming. If you lose the other — if you are so aware that you are "just pretending" that you don't genuinely inhabit the character — the drama is empty and unconvincing.

The skill of drama is maintaining both simultaneously: being genuinely moved by what the character experiences, while remaining yourself as the artist who is making conscious choices about how to express that experience.

📖The Lesson — The Art of Spontaneous Theatre

What Is Improvisation?

Improvisation (or "improv") is the art of creating theatre in the moment — no script, no rehearsal, no plan. Characters, stories, relationships, and worlds emerge spontaneously from the interaction between performers and, in live improv, between performers and audience.

Improv is ancient — much of the performance at medieval fairs and circuses was improvised. The Commedia dell'Arte tradition of Italy (16th–18th centuries) featured set character types (the clever servant, the pompous old man, the young lovers) and standard situations, but the specific dialogue and action were created fresh in each performance.

The Principles of Improv

1. "Yes, And": You've already learned this. Accept your partner's reality and build on it. This is the foundational agreement of improv: I will treat whatever you create as real, and I will add to it.

2. Listen First: Most people, when waiting for their turn to speak, are preparing their next line instead of listening to what's happening. True improv requires listening so completely that you have no plan — your response emerges from genuinely hearing your partner.

3. Make Offers: An "offer" is anything you do or say that creates something for your partner to respond to. "The sky is turning red" is an offer. "I think I hear footsteps" is an offer. Good improvisers are generous with offers — they create interesting things for their partners to work with.

4. Commit: Commitment means doing your character and your choices FULLY — no half-heartedness, no hedging. A half-committed improv choice kills scenes. A fully committed ridiculous choice can be the most interesting thing in the room.

5. Serve the Scene: In improv, the scene is more important than any individual performer. If you're trying to be funny or impressive, you're thinking about yourself. If you're focused on what the scene needs, you're thinking about the story — and the story will be better.

Second City, an improv comedy troupe founded in Chicago in 1959, has launched more comedy careers than almost any institution in history. Alumni include Tina Fey, Stephen Colbert, Bill Murray, Gilda Radner, Mike Myers, Dan Aykroyd, and hundreds more. Many of them credit their training in improv principles — especially "Yes, And" and listening — as foundational not just to their comedy but to their approach to life and collaboration.

Constraints Make You Creative

One of the great discoveries of improvisation is that constraints make you more creative, not less. When you can do anything, the blank slate is paralyzing. When you have a specific rule — "every sentence must begin with the next letter of the alphabet" or "you can only speak in questions" or "you are a character who is afraid of chairs" — the constraint forces your brain to find creative solutions within a defined space.

This is why the best improv games have specific rules. The rules are not obstacles — they are the fuel that drives creativity.

📖The Lesson — Conflict Is Story

The Formula Every Story Uses

Here is the secret formula hidden in virtually every story ever told:

CHARACTER + WANT + OBSTACLE = STORY

A character wants something. Something stands in the way of getting it. That's your story. The rest — plot, setting, theme, dialogue — is how this simple formula unfolds.

The Four Types of Conflict

1. Person vs. Person: The character's goal is blocked by another character. This is the most common type because it allows for dialogue, negotiation, and dramatic confrontation. Examples: two siblings fighting over a toy; a hero facing a villain; two old friends who now want different things.

2. Person vs. Nature: The character's goal is blocked by forces in the natural world: a storm, an illness, wilderness, drought, an animal. These stories often explore human resilience and our relationship with the natural world. Example: a child lost in a forest at night.

3. Person vs. Society: The character's goal is blocked by social rules, laws, expectations, or systems. These are often the most politically interesting stories. Examples: a person from a marginalized community fighting for recognition; a character who must break an unjust rule to do what is right.

4. Person vs. Self: The most psychologically complex type — the character's goal is blocked by their own fears, beliefs, or past. The conflict is entirely internal. Example: a character who knows what they should do but is too afraid to do it.

The Lion King is based on Shakespeare's Hamlet, which is based on a Scandinavian legend. Hamlet is a nearly perfect example of Person vs. Self conflict: Hamlet knows exactly what he needs to do (confront his uncle) but cannot bring himself to do it. The entire play — all five acts — is about this inner conflict. Person vs. Self conflicts often produce the most profound drama because they mirror the interior struggles that real humans actually face.

Dramatic Structure

Most Western drama follows a five-part structure first described by the German playwright Gustav Freytag in 1863. It is often called Freytag's Pyramid:

  • Exposition: We meet the characters, learn the setting, and understand the world before the conflict begins. This must be brief — too much setup and the audience loses interest before anything happens.
  • Rising Action: The conflict begins and complications build. Each new event makes the situation more complex and the stakes higher. The character wants their goal more desperately; the obstacles multiply.
  • Climax: The moment of maximum tension. The conflict reaches its peak. Everything has led to this moment. In a well-constructed drama, the audience feels that this moment could go either way — the outcome is genuinely uncertain.
  • Falling Action: The result of the climax plays out. The tension releases.
  • Resolution: A new order is established. Things are different from the beginning. Something has been gained or lost. The world of the story is changed.

Notice that the resolution is NOT necessarily happy. Many great dramas end tragically. What matters is that the resolution feels TRUE — earned by everything that came before it. An ending that doesn't fit the story it follows is called a deus ex machina (Latin: "god from the machine") — a resolution that comes out of nowhere and solves things without having been earned.

📖The Lesson — From Page to Stage

What Is a Script?

A script is a written text that contains the dialogue (spoken words), stage directions (instructions for movement and action), and sometimes descriptions of the setting and characters. A script is not a finished artwork — it is a blueprint. The finished artwork is the performance.

This is a crucial distinction: a script on paper, unperformed, is like sheet music unplayed — it contains the work in potential, but it is not yet the work itself. The work exists only in the act of performance.

What the Script Cannot Tell You

A script tells you what characters say. It does not tell you:

  • How they say it (the vocal quality, pace, pitch, volume)
  • What they're thinking when they say it (the subtext)
  • Where they're standing or how they're moving (usually)
  • What they feel in their bodies
  • What happened to them before the scene begins
  • What they want
  • What they're afraid of

All of this — everything that makes a performance alive — must be supplied by the actors and director. This is why two productions of the same play can be completely different experiences. The text is the same; the performance decisions make them different works of art.

Subtext: What Is Really Being Said

Subtext is one of the most powerful concepts in acting. It refers to the thoughts, feelings, and intentions beneath what a character actually says. In real life, people rarely speak their subtext directly. We say "I'm not hungry" when we're actually deeply upset. We say "Whatever you want to do" when we strongly prefer one thing over another.

The Russian playwright Anton Chekhov (1860–1904) was a master of subtext. His plays are full of characters who talk about trivial things — the weather, lunch, old memories — while the audience understands that they are actually expressing grief, longing, love, or despair. The drama lives entirely in what is not said.

The Production Team

Theatre is a collaborative art form. A production involves many different roles:

  • Playwright: Writes the script
  • Director: Interprets the script and guides the actors and designers to realize a specific vision of the play
  • Actor: Inhabits characters and brings them to life through voice, body, and imagination
  • Set Designer: Creates the visual world of the play — what the stage looks like
  • Costume Designer: Creates what the characters wear, expressing their personality and status through clothing
  • Lighting Designer: Controls how the stage is lit — time of day, mood, focus
  • Sound Designer: Creates the audio environment — sound effects, music, atmosphere
  • Stage Manager: Coordinates all these elements to ensure the production runs smoothly
Tomson Highway, an Anishinaabe/Cree playwright from Ontario, is one of Canada's most celebrated dramatists. His play "The Rez Sisters" (1986) brought the voices and experiences of First Nations women to Canadian theatre for the first time in a major way. He wrote it while struggling with the pain of residential school trauma — turning personal and communal suffering into profound, humorous, and deeply human theatre. He has described playwriting as a spiritual practice.

📖The Lesson — A World of Drama

Theatre Everywhere

Like dance, drama appears in every human culture. But unlike dance, dramatic traditions often have complex, specific forms with centuries of development, specific training methods, specific stories, and specific cultural functions.

Ancient Greek Theatre (c. 500–400 BCE)

Greek theatre is the foundation of Western theatre. Performed in enormous open-air amphitheatres, Greek plays were part of religious festivals honoring the god Dionysus. The audience sat on stone seats carved into hillsides (remarkable acoustics — whispers on stage could be heard clearly at the back). All actors wore masks (persona) that exaggerated their characters' traits. There was always a chorus — a group of actors who sang, danced, and commented on the action. Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides were the three great tragedians; Aristophanes wrote wildly satirical comedies. Many of the stories told — Oedipus, Medea, Antigone — are still performed around the world today.

Japanese Noh and Kabuki Theatre

Noh (developed 14th century): One of the world's most refined and slow theatrical forms. Noh performances can last many hours. The movement is extremely stylized and slow — a single step can take minutes. Masks are carved by master craftspeople and convey complex emotional states. Music, poetry, and movement combine in strict formal patterns. Noh is meditative, spiritual, and requires complete focused attention from the audience.

Kabuki (developed 17th century): Wild, colourful, spectacular theatre with elaborate costumes, dramatic makeup (kumadori), acrobatic movement, and music. Kabuki mixes tragedy and comedy. It was originally performed by women (later banned by the government) and is now performed entirely by men. Famous for sudden dramatic moments called "mie" — where an actor strikes a pose and the audience shouts their approval.

Indian Classical Drama — Kathakali

From the state of Kerala in India, Kathakali is one of the world's most complex performance traditions. Performers train from childhood in precise movement systems. The face is painted in elaborate geometric patterns (each pattern indicates the character type). Stories are taken from Hindu epics like the Mahabharata and Ramayana. Performances traditionally begin at night and last until sunrise. The hand gestures (mudras) — each with a specific meaning — form an entire language; there are 24 major mudras with multiple sub-gestures, capable of expressing every concept in the universe according to its tradition.

Shadow Puppetry

Found across Indonesia (Wayang Kulit), China, India, Thailand, and Turkey, shadow puppetry creates drama through flat puppets held behind a lit screen. The puppets are elaborately cut from leather or hide, casting intricate shadows. In Javanese Wayang Kulit, a single dalang (master puppeteer) manipulates all the puppets, provides all the voices, conducts the orchestra, and narrates — often for 8–9 hours straight. This solo control of an entire world is considered one of the world's most demanding performance arts.

Indigenous Drama in Canada

Contemporary Indigenous theatre in Canada is one of the most vibrant and important parts of our national cultural landscape. Companies like Native Earth Performing Arts (the oldest Indigenous theatre company in Canada, founded 1982 in Toronto) produce work that is simultaneously rooted in traditional storytelling and engaged with contemporary issues. Playwrights like Drew Hayden Taylor (Anishinaabe) and Marie Clements (Métis) create work that explores residential school trauma, Indigenous land rights, family, humor, and resilience — work that is distinctly Indigenous AND distinctly Canadian AND world-class theatre by any standard.

The Stratford Festival in Ontario, Canada — founded in 1952 — is one of the leading Shakespeare festivals in the world. But it also produces new Canadian plays, musicals, and increasingly work by Indigenous and BIPOC playwrights. It draws over 500,000 visitors a year to a small town in southwestern Ontario and has launched hundreds of major theatrical careers including those of Christopher Plummer and Colm Feore.

The Drama of Real Life

Theatre scholar Peter Brook (born 1925) wrote: "I can take any empty space and call it a bare stage. A man walks across this empty space whilst someone else is watching him, and this is all that is needed for an act of theatre to be engaged." What Brook means is that drama is not about stages, costumes, scripts, or lighting — it is about the act of one human being attending to another human being with full presence and intention. This happens everywhere, all the time.

When you listen genuinely to someone telling you about their day — really attend, notice their body language, hear the subtext beneath the words — you are doing what drama trains you to do. When you try to understand why a person acted the way they did by imagining their perspective, you are using dramatic empathy. When you tell a story, you are a storyteller. Drama is not a special activity that happens on stages — it is a way of being in the world.

🔊 Lesson 1 · What Is Sound?

Curriculum: M1.1 · M3.1

✨Wonder Hook — Can You Catch a Sound?

Close your eyes for ten seconds. Just listen. Don't peek. What do you hear? A car? Wind? Someone breathing? Your own heartbeat?

Sound is everywhere — and almost nothing is completely silent. Even in the quietest room in the world (scientists built one and people found it so uncomfortable they had to leave after minutes), your own body makes noise.

Big wonder: Where do sounds actually come from? Could you explain it to someone without using the word "sound"?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Understand that sound is produced by vibration (M1.1)
  • Identify different types of sounds in the environment (M3.1)
  • Begin using musical vocabulary: pitch, duration, dynamics, timbre
  • Describe and compare sounds using expressive language
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

Before the lesson

Gather a rubber band, a plastic ruler, an empty cardboard tube, and a metal spoon. These everyday objects will become your child's first "instrument lab." No musical training required from you — your role is co-discoverer.

Core concept to convey

Sound = vibration travelling through matter. When something shakes back and forth very quickly, it pushes the air around it, which pushes more air, creating a wave that reaches your ear. Stretch the rubber band and pluck it so your child can see it wobble — that wobble IS the sound.

If your child asks…

"Why can't I see sound waves?" — Drop a stone in a still bowl of water and show the ripples. Sound travels the same way, only in air instead of water, and too fast for eyes to follow.

Vocabulary to use naturally

vibration · pitch · timbre · duration · dynamics

📖The Lesson — Sound Is Vibration

Part 1 · Feel the Vibration

Place your fingertips gently on your throat and hum. Feel that buzzing? That is your vocal cords vibrating — your body's own musical instrument.

Now place your hand flat on a table and tap it with one finger. You'll feel a tiny shudder. Every sound you've ever heard began with something moving like that.

Part 2 · The Four Elements of Sound

Musicians describe sound using four main qualities:

  • Pitch — How high or low a sound is. A whistle is high pitch; a tuba is low pitch.
  • Duration — How long a sound lasts. A clock's tick is short; the hum of a refrigerator goes on and on.
  • Dynamics — How loud or quiet. A whisper is soft (piano in Italian); a shout is loud (forte).
  • Timbre (TAM-ber) — The colour or character of a sound. This is what makes a guitar and a flute sound different even when they play the same note.

Part 3 · Sound Hunt

Walk through your home and find five sounds. For each one, note: What made it? High or low pitch? Short or long? Loud or soft? Special character?

Part 4 · The Elastic Band Orchestra

Stretch an elastic band and pluck it. Watch it vibrate. Now stretch it tighter and pluck again — higher pitch! Looser — lower pitch. You've just discovered the relationship between tension and pitch. Guitar strings work exactly the same way.

Connection

Ontario Music Grade 1 begins by building your ear and vocabulary so that you can speak about and create music with precision. A musician who can describe sounds is a musician who can shape them.

🎵Think Like a Sound Scientist

Scientists who study sound are called acousticians. They figure out how to make concert halls sound perfect, how to reduce noise pollution in cities, and how dolphins communicate underwater.

Think: If sound is vibration, and vibration needs matter to travel through, what would happen to sound in outer space (where there is no air)?

Challenge: Design a simple instrument using only items from your recycling bin. Draw it and label which part vibrates to make sound.

🥁 Lesson 2 · Beat, Rhythm, and Tempo

Curriculum: M1.2 · M3.2

✨Wonder Hook — Your Body Already Knows Rhythm

Clap along to any song you love. Without thinking about it, you automatically find the beat. How do you do that? You've never been taught — yet your body knows.

Human beings have been feeling rhythm before they were born. A baby in the womb hears its mother's heartbeat for nine months — roughly 70 beats per minute, one of the most common tempos in popular music. Coincidence?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish between beat (steady pulse) and rhythm (long/short patterns) (M1.2)
  • Clap, tap, and move to demonstrate beat and simple rhythm patterns (M3.2)
  • Identify fast (allegro) and slow (largo) tempos by listening
  • Read and perform simple rhythmic notation (quarter note, half note, rest)
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

The key distinction

Many people confuse beat and rhythm. Here is the clearest way to explain it:

  • Beat — the steady, even pulse that never changes, like a ticking clock or a marching soldier's footsteps.
  • Rhythm — the pattern of long and short sounds layered on top of the beat. The words of a song create rhythm.

Say "Happy Birthday" slowly while tapping the beat on your knee. Your voice says "Hap-py BIRTH-day TO you" (rhythm) while your knee taps 1-2-3, 1-2-3 (beat). They are different things happening at once.

Hands-on activity

Say the word "watermelon" repeatedly while clapping the syllables: WA-ter-mel-on (4 even claps). That pattern IS a rhythm. Clap it faster and slower — you've just changed the tempo!

📖The Lesson — Steady Pulse, Lively Pattern

Part 1 · Finding the Beat

The beat is the heartbeat of music. It's always there, steady and even. Play any song and tap your foot. That tapping is the beat. Every piece of music, from a lullaby to a rock song, has one — even if it's very slow or very fast.

Part 2 · Rhythm Rides the Beat

Now sing "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star" and clap every syllable: Twin-kle Twin-kle Lit-tle Star. That uneven pattern (some short, some long) is the rhythm. Rhythm dances on top of the beat.

Part 3 · Tempo

Tempo means how fast or slow the beat moves. Musicians use Italian words:

  • Largo — very slow, like slow motion walking
  • Andante — medium, walking pace
  • Allegro — fast and lively
  • Presto — very fast, almost a run

Listen to a lullaby and then to an upbeat pop song. Can you name their tempos?

Part 4 · Simple Rhythm Notation

Music has a written language. A quarter note (♩) lasts one beat. A half note (𝅗𝅥) lasts two beats. A rest (𝄽) means silence for one beat. Silence is part of music too!

Try clapping this pattern: ♩ ♩ 𝅗𝅥 | ♩ ♩ 𝅗𝅥 — that's the start of "Hot Cross Buns"!

Part 5 · Body Percussion Ensemble

Create a four-beat body percussion pattern using three sounds: clap, pat knees, stomp. For example: clap-pat-pat-stomp. Practise until you can do it without stopping. You've made a rhythm ostinato (a repeating pattern)!

🎵Think Like a Composer

Composers think about how rhythm creates feel. A steady march rhythm makes people want to walk. A swinging jazz rhythm makes them want to sway. A driving 4/4 rock beat makes them want to jump.

Think: If you were composing music for a cat sneaking up on a mouse, what tempo and rhythm pattern would you choose? What about music for an elephant slowly wading into a river?

Experiment: Say your full name (first and last) as a rhythm. Can you clap it? Is it fast or slow? Does it feel like a march, a waltz, or something else?

🖊️Classwork

  • 𝅗𝅥 = 2 beats (long)
  • 𝄽 = 1 beat silence

🎵 Lesson 3 · Pitch and Melody — High and Low

Curriculum: M1.3 · M3.3

✨Wonder Hook — How Do Birds Choose Their Song?

Every bird species has its own song — a specific set of pitches arranged in a unique pattern. A robin's song sounds nothing like a sparrow's. Scientists have mapped bird songs on the same musical grids we use for human music and discovered some birds sing incredibly complex compositions.

Young birds learn their song from their parents — like children learn language. But some birds are "song thieves" that copy other species. Is that still music if you borrowed someone else's melody?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Define pitch as the highness or lowness of a sound (M1.3)
  • Recognize high, medium, and low pitches by ear and by notation (M3.3)
  • Understand that melody is a series of pitches arranged in sequence
  • Sing simple melodies using correct pitch matching (solfège: do re mi fa sol)
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

What is pitch, exactly?

Pitch is determined by how fast something vibrates. A taut guitar string vibrates more times per second than a loose one — producing a higher pitch. Middle C on a piano vibrates 261.6 times per second (261.6 Hz). The A above it vibrates 440 times per second — almost twice as fast.

Solfège — the do-re-mi system

The syllables do-re-mi-fa-sol-la-ti-do (from The Sound of Music!) are the Western solfège system. They help children internalize pitch relationships before they read notation. Sing "Do Re Mi" from The Sound of Music with your child to introduce them.

Activity

Fill 7 identical glasses with increasing amounts of water. Tap each gently with a pencil. The fuller the glass, the lower the pitch. Try to tune them to a simple scale so you can play "Twinkle Twinkle."

Singing tips

If your child struggles with pitch matching, have them put their hand on their head when the note is high and touch the ground when it's low. The physical gesture helps the ear calibrate.

📖The Lesson — The Staircase of Sound

Part 1 · High, Low, and In Between

Imagine a staircase. The bottom step is the lowest, deepest sound. The top step is the highest, thinnest sound. In music, we call each step a pitch. A piano has 88 steps. A human voice has maybe 20 or 30 comfortable steps.

Part 2 · The Musical Alphabet

Western music uses just seven letter names for pitches: A, B, C, D, E, F, G. Then they repeat — A again, but higher. This higher A sounds almost the same as the lower one because it vibrates exactly twice as fast. We call this gap an octave (from the Latin word for "eight").

Part 3 · What Is a Melody?

A melody is a series of pitches played one after another — the "tune" you hum or whistle. Melodies can go up (ascending), go down (descending), or jump around. The melody is what makes one song different from another.

Part 4 · Solfège Warm-Up

Learn the do-re-mi ladder by singing each syllable while showing the pitch level with your hand (low for do, higher for each step up):

do · re · mi · fa · sol · la · ti · do

Now try singing "Mary Had a Little Lamb" on solfège syllables: mi re do re mi mi mi…

Part 5 · Melodic Direction

Draw the melodic shape of "Twinkle Twinkle" as a line graph — dots connected by lines, going up when the melody rises and down when it falls. You've made what musicians call a melodic contour map.

🎵Think Like a Composer

Composers choose pitches deliberately. A melody that keeps climbing higher can feel exciting or suspenseful. One that falls lower can feel peaceful or sad. One that stays on the same pitch can feel hypnotic or steady.

Think: What kind of melodic shape would you use to musically describe a rocket launching? A leaf falling? A heartbeat?

Singing experiment: Sing any five notes in a row going UP. Now sing five notes going DOWN. Which felt more exciting? Which felt more restful? Why?

🖊️Classwork

Choose two songs you know. For each one, draw its melodic contour as a line graph (like a mountain range): up = pitch goes up, down = pitch goes down, flat = pitch stays the same. Label the highest point and lowest point on each graph.

The "Dorian mode" was thought to make warriors brave. Do you think music can actually change who you are?

🎼 Absolute pitch (sometimes called "perfect pitch") is the ability to identify any pitch by ear without a reference note. Only about 1 in 10,000 people have it, and it's most easily developed before age 6. Some languages with tonal structure (like Mandarin) seem to produce more people with absolute pitch. 🔉 Lesson 4 · Dynamics — Loud and Soft

Curriculum: M1.4 · M3.4

✨Wonder Hook — Why Does Quiet Music Feel Louder?

Here is a strange thing: sometimes the quietest moment in a piece of music is the most powerful. In Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, after all the crashing thunder of sound, there is a moment of near-silence that makes audiences lean forward in their seats. The quiet doesn't feel small — it feels enormous.

Why? How can less be more?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Define dynamics as the variation in loudness in music (M1.4)
  • Identify and perform forte (loud), piano (soft), and mezzo levels (M3.4)
  • Understand how composers use dynamics for emotional expression
  • Apply dynamic markings (f, mf, mp, p) to performance and composition
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

Italian dynamics vocabulary

All Western music uses Italian terms for dynamics (because Italian composers dominated music publishing when these terms were standardized):

  • pp — pianissimo (very soft)
  • p — piano (soft)
  • mp — mezzo-piano (medium soft)
  • mf — mezzo-forte (medium loud)
  • f — forte (loud)
  • ff — fortissimo (very loud)

Crescendo and decrescendo

Music also moves between dynamics. A crescendo gradually gets louder (<). A decrescendo or diminuendo gradually gets softer (>). These dynamic changes are as important as the static levels.

Activity idea

Have your child read a favourite story aloud twice: once entirely in a flat monotone, once with dynamic variation — whispering scary parts, getting louder at exciting moments. Discuss how dynamics changed the experience.

📖The Lesson — The Volume of Feeling

Part 1 · What Are Dynamics?

Dynamics are the loudness and softness in music. Unlike volume on a speaker (which you can control with a dial), dynamics in live music are controlled by the performers themselves — how hard they blow, bow, or strike their instrument.

Part 2 · Dynamics Create Emotion

Think about how a story sounds when read aloud. When something scary happens, a good storyteller might whisper. When something exciting happens, they get louder. Music works exactly the same way — composers write in dynamics to guide the feelings of their audience.

Part 3 · The Dynamic Scale

Practise each level with your voice on the syllable "ah":

  1. pp — barely a breath
  2. p — a quiet speaking voice
  3. mp — a normal speaking voice
  4. mf — a confident speaking voice
  5. f — a loud classroom voice
  6. ff — a shout (use sparingly!)

Part 4 · The Crescendo Experience

Sing or hum any note at pp level. Slowly, over ten seconds, grow to ff without changing pitch. That smooth gradual increase is a crescendo. Now do the reverse (decrescendo). This technique is one of music's most powerful emotional tools.

Part 5 · Listening Challenge

Listen to Maurice Ravel's "Boléro" — it starts almost inaudibly and builds in one long crescendo for 15 minutes to a thundering finish. It's one of the most famous uses of dynamics in all of music. How does your body feel by the end?

🎵Think Like a Conductor

A conductor's job is partly to control dynamics in an orchestra. Their hands grow larger for louder passages and shrink to tiny gestures for soft ones. Without saying a word, they can shape the emotional arc of an entire symphony.

Think: If you were composing a piece of music to describe a thunderstorm, what dynamic journey would you take the listener on — from beginning (before the storm) to end (after it passes)?

Conduct an experiment: Tell a one-minute story using only hand gestures and humming — no words. Use large/loud gestures for dramatic moments and small/quiet ones for gentle moments. Have someone guess what happened in your story.

🖊️Classwork

Dynamic Notation Practise

Mark up a familiar song (write out the lyrics of "Twinkle Twinkle" or any song you know) with dynamic markings. Put a p where you want the music to be soft, an f where you want it loud, and draw a crescendo symbol where you want it to grow. Then perform it with your voice, following your markings!

🎤 Lesson 5 · Singing and Our Voices

Curriculum: M1.5

✨Wonder Hook — The World's First Instrument

Before any instrument was built, before anyone carved a bone flute or stretched a skin over a drum — there was the human voice. Every culture in the history of the world has used the voice for music. It is the one instrument we are all born with.

But here's the mystery: we don't really "see" how the voice works. It's hidden inside your throat. How exactly do you make your voice go higher or lower, louder or softer, or sound different from everyone else's?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Use speaking, singing, whispering, and chanting voices appropriately (M1.5)
  • Understand how the voice works as a physical instrument
  • Practise correct posture and breath support for singing
  • Match pitch while singing simple songs, rounds, and echo songs
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

How the voice works

The voice is produced by two small folds of tissue (vocal cords/folds) in the larynx (voice box). When air passes through them, they vibrate. Faster vibration = higher pitch. The shape of the mouth, tongue, and lips sculpts the sound into vowels and consonants.

Head voice vs. chest voice

Singing voices have two main "registers": chest voice (the lower speaking-like range, where you feel vibration in your chest) and head voice (the higher, lighter range, where you feel it in your head). Children naturally have a bright, pure head voice — encourage it! Never push a child to sing with a loud "forced" tone; healthy singing is free and easy.

Singing games to try

  • Echo singing — You sing a 4-note phrase, child echoes it back
  • Telephone pitch — Hum a note, child matches it with eyes closed
  • Siren — Slide voice from lowest comfortable pitch to highest and back down

Never

Never tell a child they "can't sing." Every child can develop their voice. If pitch matching is difficult, it simply means they need more gentle practice.

📖The Lesson — Your Built-In Instrument

Part 1 · Four Voice Types

Humans use four different types of voice depending on context:

  • Speaking voice — everyday conversation
  • Singing voice — smooth, sustained, pitch-matched
  • Whispering voice — no vocal cord vibration, just air
  • Chanting voice — speech on a steady pitch or pattern

Each voice type has its place in music. Many folk traditions use chant (like Gregorian chant or Indigenous vocal traditions). Modern music uses all four.

Part 2 · Posture for Singing

Good singing starts before you open your mouth:

  • Stand (or sit) tall — imagine a string pulling the top of your head up
  • Relax shoulders — let them drop, not scrunch
  • Feet hip-width apart if standing
  • Breathe from the belly (diaphragm), not the shoulders

Place a hand on your belly and breathe in. Your hand should move forward, not upward. If your shoulders rise when you breathe, practise belly breathing for 2 minutes.

Part 3 · The Siren Warm-Up

Gently slide your voice from your lowest comfortable pitch to your highest (like a police siren: "wooooo"). Then slide down. Repeat three times. This "siren" connects your chest voice to your head voice smoothly — the same exercise professional singers use.

Part 4 · Echo Song

A parent or sibling sings a short 4-note phrase; you echo it back identically. Do 8 rounds, gradually expanding the phrases. This is one of the best ear-training exercises for children and it's been used in music education for centuries.

Part 5 · Singing Traditions

Every culture sings. The Inuit katajjaq (throat singing) creates layered sounds two singers make simultaneously. West African griot singers are living libraries of history. Indigenous songs carry medicines, maps, and ceremonies. Opera singers can be heard without microphones in a 3,000-seat theatre because of their breath technique. All voices are extraordinary.

🎵Think Like a Vocal Coach

A vocal coach helps singers develop their voice — not by changing who they are, but by helping their natural voice come out more freely. Every singer sounds unique because of the shape of their body, mouth, and nasal passages.

Think: Your voice sounds different to you than it does to everyone else. This is because when you speak or sing, you also hear your voice conducted through the bones of your skull (bone conduction). When you hear a recording of your voice, you hear what others hear. How does this make you think about how we perceive ourselves versus how others perceive us?

Vocal exploration: Try to make 10 completely different sounds with just your voice — not words, not singing, just sounds. List each one.

🖊️Classwork

Song Performance

Choose one of these songs to perform with full attention to vocal quality: "This Land Is Your Land," "You Are My Sunshine," "O Canada," or a song from your own cultural tradition. Practise it three times. On the third run-through, focus on one thing: smooth breath support. Can you sing whole phrases without gasping for air mid-word?

🪘 Lesson 6 · Playing Simple Instruments

Curriculum: M1.6

✨Wonder Hook — A Forest of Instruments

There are an estimated 1,500 different musical instruments in the world, from the Armenian duduk (a 3,000-year-old reed flute) to the theremin (an electronic instrument you play without touching it, by moving your hands near two antennae) to the glass armonica (invented by Benjamin Franklin, played by touching spinning glass bowls).

Every one of them was invented to solve a problem: how do I make a beautiful sound? What kind of instrument would YOU invent?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Classify instruments by family: percussion, string, wind, keyboard (M1.6)
  • Demonstrate proper technique on classroom percussion instruments
  • Understand how each instrument family produces sound
  • Create and perform a simple accompaniment pattern on an instrument
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

The four instrument families

  • Percussion — struck, shaken, or scraped: drum, tambourine, triangle, marimba, xylophone
  • String — vibrating strings: violin, guitar, harp, piano (strings struck by hammers), bass
  • Wind — vibrating air column. Two sub-families: woodwinds (flute, clarinet, saxophone) and brass (trumpet, trombone, tuba)
  • Keyboard — sometimes its own family: piano, organ, harpsichord, accordion

Classroom percussion to find

Most Canadian elementary classrooms have: hand drums, rhythm sticks, egg shakers, triangles, tambourines, maracas, wood blocks, and xylophones. If you have any of these at home, this is a great time to bring them out. If not, homemade instruments work perfectly (see classwork activity).

Proper technique matters

For hand drum: strike near the edge, not the centre, for the clearest sound. Hold the mallet loosely so it can bounce back. For shakers: the sound comes from the controlled shake, not from slamming. Relaxed hands always produce better sound than tense ones.

📖The Lesson — The Orchestra of the World

Part 1 · How Instruments Make Sound

Every instrument works by making something vibrate:

  • Percussion — you strike a surface or shake a container, making the material itself vibrate
  • String — you pluck, bow, or strike a string, which vibrates back and forth
  • Wind — you blow air through or across a tube, making the air column inside vibrate
  • Keyboard — usually a mechanism that strikes a string or vibrates a reed when a key is pressed

Part 2 · The Symphony Orchestra

A full symphony orchestra has around 80–100 musicians. The sections sit in a specific arrangement: strings in front (violins, violas, cellos, basses), woodwinds in the middle (flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons), brass behind them (horns, trumpets, trombones, tuba), and percussion at the back (timpani, snare, xylophone, gong).

The conductor stands in the middle front and coordinates all 100 of them using only a baton and hand gestures.

Part 3 · Tone Production

How you play an instrument changes its tone (timbre). A drum struck in the centre sounds dull and thuddy. Struck near the edge, it rings. A guitar string plucked near the bridge sounds bright; near the sound hole, warm. Every professional musician studies how to find the best sound their instrument can make.

Part 4 · Simple Accompaniment Patterns

An accompaniment is the background pattern that supports a melody. On a hand drum or rhythm sticks, a simple 4-beat accompaniment for any song is: beat 1 strong, beats 2-3-4 soft (like a heartbeat with emphasis on 1). Try this while someone sings "Happy Birthday."

Part 5 · World Instruments Listening

Listen to (YouTube with a parent): a kora (West African 21-string harp-lute), a sitar (Indian string instrument), a didgeridoo (Australian Aboriginal wind instrument), and a tabla (Indian hand drum pair). What family does each belong to? How does each one make its unique sound?

🎵Think Like an Instrument Maker

Instrument makers (called luthiers for stringed instruments, woodwind makers, etc.) spend years perfecting their craft. The shape, thickness, and material of every part affects the sound. A Stradivarius violin, made in the 1700s, is worth millions of dollars because no one has been able to fully replicate its exact sound.

Think: If you had to make an instrument using only things you could find in your kitchen, what would you build? What family would it belong to? How would you change its pitch?

Classify: Look around your home and find 5 objects that make sound. Which instrument family does each one belong to?

👂 Lesson 7 · Listening and Responding to Music

Curriculum: M2.1 · M2.2

✨Wonder Hook — Music Without Words

In 1812, Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky wrote an overture to celebrate Russia's victory over Napoleon. He used the French national anthem (to represent Napoleon's army), the Russian national anthem (to represent Russia), and actual cannon fire in the score. The piece is called the 1812 Overture.

No words are spoken — but the whole story is told in sound. People who have never read any history of this battle say they can feel a battle happening when they hear it.

How does music tell stories without words?

👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

What musical listening teaches

Active listening is a learnable skill. Most people hear music but don't truly listen to it. Teaching your child to listen actively — to notice specific musical elements — develops focus, vocabulary, empathy, and the ability to connect abstract experience (emotion) with concrete causes (musical structure).

Two pieces to prepare

For this lesson, choose two contrasting pieces:

  1. Something energetic: Beethoven's 5th Symphony (opening), "Flight of the Bumblebee" (Rimsky-Korsakov), or any upbeat piece your child enjoys
  2. Something gentle: Debussy's "Clair de Lune," Chopin's "Nocturne in E♭," or a lullaby

How to guide listening

Before playing, tell your child: "We're going to listen with our whole body. Your job is just to notice." After each piece, ask open questions: What colour did you see? What animal did you think of? What happened in your body?

📖The Lesson — The Art of Listening

Part 1 · Active vs. Passive Listening

There are two ways to hear music. Passive listening is background music while you do other things — your brain filters it out. Active listening means stopping, facing the music, and paying full attention. Active listeners notice things passive listeners completely miss.

Part 2 · Layers of Listening

Professional musicians and music critics listen in layers:

  1. Layer 1 — Emotional: How does this make me feel? What images come to mind?
  2. Layer 2 — Structural: What is the tempo, dynamics, and instrumentation? Does it change?
  3. Layer 3 — Analytical: Why do those choices create those feelings? What specifically makes this music happy, scary, or sad?

Part 3 · Programme Music

Music that tells a specific story or paints a scene is called programme music. Famous examples include:

  • Vivaldi's "The Four Seasons" (violin concertos depicting spring, summer, autumn, winter)
  • Saint-Saëns's "Carnival of the Animals" (each section depicts a different animal)
  • Prokofiev's "Peter and the Wolf" (each character is represented by a different instrument)

Part 4 · The Responding Body

Your body responds to music physically. Goosebumps (frisson) appear when music gives you chills. Tears can arise from music even if you don't know why. Feet tap. Heads nod. These physical responses are not just habits — they are your nervous system responding to music's structure.

Part 5 · Comparing Two Pieces

Listen to your two chosen pieces back to back. Compare: tempo (fast/slow), dynamics (loud/soft), instrumentation (how many instruments? which ones?), mood (one word for each), and melodic movement (mostly going up? down? staying the same?).

🎵Think Like a Music Critic

Music critics write about music for newspapers, magazines, and websites. They must explain in words what sound makes people feel — one of the hardest things to write about. A great music review helps a reader understand why a piece matters, even if they've never heard it.

Think: After listening to your two chosen pieces, write three sentences describing each one as if you are writing for a newspaper. What musical choices make it unique? What feeling does it create and why?

Challenge: Listen to a third, unfamiliar piece. Without knowing anything about it, describe what you think is happening in the "story" of the music.

🌍 Lesson 8 · Music Around the World

✨Wonder Hook — Music Finds a Way

In every corner of the world — in rainforests, deserts, Arctic tundra, mountain valleys, and bustling cities — humans make music. They use whatever materials they find: shells, bones, clay, wood, stretched animal skins, metal, plastic bottles.

📖The Lesson — The World's Playlist

Part 1 · Music Has a Job

In every culture, music does something — it isn't just entertainment. Some music is sacred and only performed in ceremonies. Some is for work (sea shanties helped sailors coordinate their rowing; field songs helped enslaved workers endure and communicate). Some preserves history (Indigenous oral traditions, griots in West Africa). Some heals (sound healing in many traditions). Some declares resistance (protest songs, freedom songs).

Part 2 · Music in Canada

Canada is one of the world's most musically diverse countries. In a single Canadian city you might hear:

  • Indigenous traditional music — drumming, singing, and ceremonial songs from First Nations, Métis, and Inuit communities
  • African-Canadian music — Caribbean soca and calypso, reggae, Afrobeats, jazz, R&B
  • South Asian music — Bollywood, Carnatic classical, bhangra
  • Celtic traditions — brought by Irish and Scottish settlers, now with Canadian flair
  • Québécois folk — fiddle music, foot stomping, joual folk songs
  • Contemporary Canadian pop — from Celine Dion to Drake to The Weeknd to Alanis Morissette

Part 3 · Scales of the World

Western music typically uses scales of 12 notes (the chromatic scale) and often 7-note major or minor scales. But:

  • Arabic music uses quarter tones — pitches between the notes of a piano, creating a distinctive wavering quality
  • Indian classical music uses 72 parent scales (melakarta ragas) — far more than Western music's two (major and minor)
  • Balinese gamelan music uses scales tuned differently on every island — no two gamelan ensembles are tuned exactly alike
  • Blues music created the blue notes — bent, in-between pitches that defined a new style of music that changed the world

Part 4 · Drum Traditions

The drum is perhaps the most universal instrument. The West African djembe, the Japanese taiko, the Irish bodhrán, the Aboriginal Australian didgeridoo (which creates rhythm through circular breathing), the North Indian tabla, and First Nations hand drums are all from different traditions — but all connect communities through rhythm.

Part 5 · Your Musical Heritage

Where does your family come from? What music is associated with that place and culture? Learning the music of your heritage is one of the most powerful ways to stay connected to where you come from.

🎵Think Like a Musicologist

A musicologist studies music as a cultural, historical, and social phenomenon. They ask: Why did this music develop here? What does it tell us about the people who created it? How has it changed over time?

Think: When Western settlers arrived in Canada, they often tried to suppress Indigenous music and ceremonies. Why would colonizers target music specifically? What does that tell you about the power of music?

Connection: Your family has a musical tradition — even if it's just the songs your family knows. What is one song that feels like it belongs to your family? Where does it come from?

🎨 Lesson 1 · What Is Art? Elements and Principles

Curriculum: V1.1 · V3.1

✨Wonder Hook — When Did Humans First Make Art?

Deep inside a cave in Spain, on a wall that no one could reach without crawling through narrow passages in complete darkness, someone painted a collection of red hand outlines 64,000 years ago. The person who painted them was not a Homo sapiens — they were a Neanderthal. Before modern humans even arrived in Europe, our ancient cousins were making art.

Why? They had no electricity, no extra time, and survival was dangerous. Yet they crawled into a dangerous cave just to put their hand on a wall and blow red pigment around it. What did it mean to them?

Your big question: Why do you think humans — even ancient ones — felt the need to make art?

👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

What you're building

This first lesson establishes the entire visual vocabulary your child will use all year. Think of the seven elements of art like the seven letters of a secret code — once your child knows them, they can "read" any artwork they encounter. Your goal today is not mastery; it's familiarity. You want your child leaving this lesson able to point at an artwork and name at least two elements they can see.

How to introduce the lesson

Before reading, do this together: pull up any famous painting on a phone or computer (the Mona Lisa, Starry Night, or anything you both like). Ask: "What do you see?" Accept any answer. Then say: "Artists make choices about every part of a painting. Today we're going to learn the seven choices every artist makes — they're called the elements of art."

Teaching the elements

As you read each element, physically point to an example in the artwork you chose. For "line" — point to actual visible lines. For "colour" — point to a dominant colour. Don't rush; dwell on each one for 60 seconds.

Your child has understood this lesson when they can: (1) name all seven elements from memory, (2) point to examples of at least four in any artwork, (3) explain the difference between shape (2D) and form (3D).

📖The Lesson — The Artist's Toolkit

Part 1 · What IS Visual Art?

Visual art is art you experience with your eyes. It includes drawing, painting, printmaking, sculpture, photography, collage, and digital art. Unlike music (which you hear) or dance (which you see in motion), visual art is usually still — it captures a single moment, idea, or feeling in a permanent form.

Art has been made for thousands of years, on every continent, by every culture. It has been used to record history, tell stories, honour the dead, communicate with gods, celebrate beauty, and ask difficult questions about what it means to be human.

Part 2 · The Seven Elements of Art

Every visual artwork — from a Grade 1 drawing to the Sistine Chapel ceiling — is made using the same seven building blocks. Artists call them the elements of art.

1. Line — A mark made by a moving point. Lines can be straight or curved, thick or thin, broken or continuous. They can create movement, define edges, and suggest emotion (jagged lines feel agitated; wavy lines feel calm).

2. Shape — A two-dimensional (flat) area enclosed by lines. Shapes can be geometric (circles, squares, triangles — precise and mathematical) or organic (irregular, flowing, like shapes in nature — a leaf, a cloud, a puddle).

3. Form — Like shape, but three-dimensional. A circle becomes a sphere. A square becomes a cube. Form has depth you can walk around. Sculpture creates form.

4. Colour — One of the most powerful elements. Colour has three properties: hue (the colour itself: red, blue, yellow), value (how light or dark it is), and intensity (how vivid or dull it is). Colours carry emotional meaning — red for passion or danger, blue for calm or sadness.

5. Value — The lightness or darkness of any colour (including black and white). Value creates the illusion of light and shadow, making flat drawings look three-dimensional.

6. Texture — The surface quality of an object, real or implied. In sculpture, you can actually feel texture. In painting, texture is suggested — a skilled artist can make you feel rough bark or soft fur using only paint on a flat canvas.

7. Space — The area around, between, and within objects. Positive space is occupied by objects; negative space is the empty area around them. Artists use space to create depth, focus, and breathing room.

Part 3 · Art Is Everywhere

Art doesn't only live in museums. Look around your home right now: the pattern on a tea towel, the shape of a lamp, the colour of your front door, the design of a cereal box — all of these involved an artist making decisions about line, shape, colour, and space. Graphic designers, architects, fashion designers, and illustrators all use the seven elements every day.

🎨Think Like an Art Historian

Art historians study artworks to understand what they meant in their time and place. They ask: Who made this? When and where? What were they trying to communicate? What choices did they make and why?

Look at any artwork (search "famous paintings" with a parent, or look at a picture book) and answer these questions using art vocabulary:

  1. What elements of art can you identify? (Name at least 4)
  2. What feeling does the artwork create in you? Which element do you think creates that feeling most strongly?
  3. If you could remove one element entirely, how would the artwork change?

Design challenge: Can you create something using only lines — no shapes filled in? Only straight lines, only curved ones, or a mix? What can you communicate using line alone?

✏️ Lesson 2 · Line and Shape — Drawing the World

Curriculum: V1.2 · V3.2

✨Wonder Hook — Can You Draw a Perfect Circle?

Try it. Put your pencil on paper and draw a circle without lifting it. Now look at it critically. Is it perfect? Almost certainly not. And yet your brain recognized it as a circle the instant you drew it.

Now here is the strange part: human beings are extremely bad at drawing perfect geometric shapes by hand — but we are very good at recognizing imperfect ones. A wobbly circle still looks like a circle. Why? And why does that matter for art?

👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

Why line and shape come first

Every drawing, painting, and design begins with line. Before an artist reaches for colour or texture, they must place marks on a surface. This lesson builds the foundational drawing skills your child will use in every visual arts lesson from now on.

The most important thing to say

Many children declare "I can't draw" by age 6 because they compare their work to adult artists or computer images. Counter this immediately and firmly: "Drawing is a learnable skill, like reading. You couldn't read at age 3. You learned. Drawing is the same." Then show them that professional artists spend years practising these exact exercises.

Contour drawing

A "blind contour" drawing is done by looking only at the subject — not at the paper — while drawing. The result looks funny, but the skill it builds (learning to really LOOK) is essential. Do it together so it feels like a game, not an assessment.

What to notice

Watch whether your child varies their line quality — thick vs. thin, smooth vs. jagged. Encourage variety: "Can you make that line feel more excited? More sleepy?"

📖The Lesson — Lines That Talk

Part 1 · Lines Are Not Equal

A line is the most basic element of art — just a moving point. But not all lines are the same. Each type of line communicates something different:

  • Straight lines — feel rigid, controlled, architectural
  • Curved lines — feel soft, gentle, natural
  • Diagonal lines — feel dynamic, moving, unstable
  • Zigzag lines — feel energetic, tense, electrical
  • Wavy lines — feel calm, flowing, rhythmic
  • Thick lines — feel bold, confident, heavy
  • Thin lines — feel delicate, precise, quiet
  • Broken lines — feel tentative, uncertain, interrupted

An artist chooses line types deliberately to create a certain feeling. A portrait of a fierce warrior uses strong diagonal and thick lines. A peaceful seascape uses gentle curves and thin, flowing lines.

Part 2 · Shapes From Lines

When lines enclose an area, they create a shape. Shapes fall into two categories:

Geometric shapes — precise, mathematical: circles, squares, rectangles, triangles, pentagons, hexagons. These shapes feel orderly, structured, human-made. Architecture, technology, and design lean heavily on geometric shapes.

Organic shapes — irregular, natural, flowing. The shape of a leaf, a stone, a cloud, a puddle, a human body. These shapes feel alive, imperfect, natural. Landscape art and nature illustration are built from organic shapes.

Part 3 · Contour Drawing

A contour drawing traces the edges and interior lines of an object. It's one of the most important drawing exercises there is. Professional artists do contour drawing exercises every day as warm-up.

Steps: Place any simple object in front of you (a shoe, a piece of fruit, a hand). Put your pencil on the paper. Now look ONLY at the object — not at your paper — and slowly trace what you see with your pencil. Your hand follows your eye. This is called "blind contour drawing" and it trains your eye to observe detail you normally skip.

Part 4 · Gesture Drawing

A gesture drawing captures the overall movement and feeling of a subject quickly — usually in 30–60 seconds. Rather than carefully tracing edges, you capture the ENERGY of the pose with quick, flowing lines. Life drawing classes at art schools begin every session with gesture drawing. It warms up your arm, loosens your grip, and teaches you to see the "big picture" before the details.

Part 5 · Composition With Shapes

A composition is the arrangement of elements in an artwork. Where you place shapes matters enormously. A shape in the centre feels stable. A shape at the edge feels like it might leave. Overlapping shapes create depth. Repeated shapes create pattern and rhythm.

Georgia O'Keeffe, one of the most important American painters of the 20th century, simplified natural forms into pure, large, powerful shapes — especially flowers and bones. Her work shows how shape alone can be deeply emotional.

🎨Think Like a Graphic Designer

Graphic designers use shapes and lines to communicate immediately — in a fraction of a second. Think about logos: Nike's swoosh (one curved line), the Apple logo (an organic shape of a bitten apple), the Olympic rings (five geometric circles). These shapes must communicate personality, trust, and identity instantly, with no words.

Design challenge: Create a logo for a fictional business of your choice (a bakery, a dog rescue, a bookshop, anything you like). Your logo may only use lines and shapes — no words, no colour. Can you make it instantly recognizable?

Analyse: Why do you think most road signs are geometric shapes (octagons, triangles, circles, rectangles) rather than organic shapes? What would happen if stop signs were leaf-shaped?

🌈 Lesson 3 · Colour — The Artist's Palette

Curriculum: V1.3 · V3.3

✨Wonder Hook — Is the Sky Really Blue?

Here is a fact that might break your brain a little: before about 2,500 years ago, almost no language in the world had a word for the colour blue. Ancient Greek texts — including Homer's epics, which describe the sea endlessly — never once call it "blue." Homer describes the sea as "wine-dark."

This doesn't mean ancient Greeks were colour-blind. It means that without a word for a colour, the brain doesn't organize it as a distinct category. If you don't have a name for it, you don't see it as separate from other colours. Language shapes perception — and art may have helped create the very words we use for colour.

Your wonder: Do you think there are colours right now that humans see but don't have words for?

👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

What you need

Ideally: red, yellow, and blue paint (tempera or watercolour), a palette or paper plate, brushes, water, and paper. If paint isn't available, coloured pencils or crayons can demonstrate colour mixing (layering colours on top of each other). Even coloured cellophane from gift bags held over each other shows subtractive colour mixing.

Key concept: subtractive vs. additive colour mixing

Paint mixes by subtractive colour: mixing all colours makes brown/black (pigments absorb light). Light mixes by additive colour: mixing red, green, and blue light makes white (the basis of phone and TV screens). This is why your screen's primary colours (RGB) are different from paint's primary colours (RYB). Don't confuse children with this unless they ask — but be ready to explain it.

Teaching the colour wheel

Draw a simple 12-slot circle together. Fill in red, yellow, blue evenly spaced (primary). Then mix each pair to get orange, green, violet (secondary). Then mix each primary with its neighbouring secondary (tertiary). This hands-on process is far more memorable than memorizing a list.

The emotion conversation

After mixing colours, show your child two images: one in warm colours (reds/oranges/yellows) and one in cool colours (blues/greens/purples). Ask: "Which feels warmer? Which feels more peaceful? Which would you paint for a bedroom and which for a kitchen? Why?"

📖The Lesson — The Language of Colour

Part 1 · The Colour Wheel

Isaac Newton was the first person to arrange colours in a circle — back in 1666. He realized that white light contains ALL colours (you can see this when a prism splits sunlight into a rainbow), and that colours relate to each other in a circular pattern.

Today, artists use the colour wheel to understand colour relationships. Here's how it works:

Primary Colours — Red, yellow, blue. These cannot be made by mixing other colours. All other colours come from combinations of these three.

Secondary Colours — Made by mixing two primaries: Red + Yellow = Orange; Yellow + Blue = Green; Blue + Red = Violet (purple).

Tertiary Colours — Made by mixing a primary with an adjacent secondary: red-orange, yellow-orange, yellow-green, blue-green, blue-violet, red-violet. These are the colours between colours.

Part 2 · Colour Properties

Every colour has three properties:

  • Hue — the name of the colour (red, blue, turquoise)
  • Value — how light or dark it is. Adding white to a colour makes a tint; adding black makes a shade
  • Intensity (saturation) — how vivid or dull it is. A pure red is high intensity; a brownish-red is low intensity

Part 3 · Warm and Cool Colours

Colours are grouped into warm and cool families:

Warm colours — reds, oranges, yellows. They feel energetic, lively, and advance visually (they seem to come closer to you). Think of fire, sunsets, autumn leaves.

Cool colours — blues, greens, purples. They feel calm, peaceful, and recede visually (they seem to move away). Think of ice, ocean, moonlight, forest shadows.

Part 4 · Colour Relationships

Complementary colours sit opposite each other on the colour wheel: red/green, blue/orange, yellow/violet. When placed next to each other, they vibrate with energy — each makes the other look more vivid. Van Gogh used complementary colours constantly to create visual excitement.

Analogous colours sit next to each other on the wheel: yellow, yellow-green, green. They create harmony and calm — nature uses analogous colours constantly (a forest is all analogous greens and browns).

Part 5 · Colour and Emotion — The Psychology

Artists (and psychologists, and marketers) know that colour affects emotion:

  • Red — passion, danger, urgency, love, power
  • Blue — calm, trust, sadness, depth, intelligence
  • Yellow — joy, energy, caution, optimism
  • Green — nature, growth, health, jealousy
  • Purple — royalty, mystery, spirituality, creativity
  • Black — sophistication, mystery, death, power
  • White — purity, simplicity, cleanliness, emptiness

But these meanings are culturally determined — in many Asian cultures, white is associated with mourning, not purity. Red in China means luck and celebration, not danger. Art travels across cultures; its colour meanings sometimes travel differently.

🎨Think Like a Colour Theorist

Josef Albers, a 20th-century artist and teacher, spent decades studying how colours affect each other. He demonstrated that colour is always relative — the same grey looks lighter on a dark background and darker on a light one. The same colour can look like two completely different colours depending on what surrounds it.

Experiment: Draw two identical medium-grey squares on separate pieces of paper. Place one on a black background. Place one on a white background. Do they look the same? This is the power of colour relationship.

Thinking challenge: If you had to paint a room to make someone feel calm and focused (a study room), what colour scheme would you use? What about a room meant to make someone feel excited and creative? Explain your colour choices using what you know.

🪨 Lesson 4 · Texture and Form — Touching Art

Curriculum: V1.4 · V3.4

✨Wonder Hook — Can You Feel a Painting?

Walk up close to a painting by Vincent van Gogh — or look at a high- resolution photograph of one online. The paint is not flat. It's piled up in thick, swirling ridges that jump off the canvas. You could almost reach out and feel the texture of the sky in "The Starry Night."

Van Gogh painted this way deliberately — using a technique called impasto (Italian for "paste"). He believed that the physical texture of paint could carry emotion that smooth paint could not.

Your question: If art is something we look at, why should texture — something we feel — matter so much?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish between actual texture (felt) and implied texture (seen) (V1.4)
  • Understand form as the three-dimensional version of shape (V1.4)
  • Create texture using rubbings, collage, and impasto-style techniques (V3.4)
  • Identify and describe texture and form in artworks and the natural world
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

Two kinds of texture

The key concept is the distinction between:

  • Actual (tactile) texture — the real surface you can feel: the roughness of bark, the smoothness of glass, the fuzziness of a peach
  • Implied (visual) texture — texture the artist creates the illusion of on a flat surface: a painting of grass that looks prickly even though the canvas is smooth

Texture rubbings — best activity for this lesson

Frottage (from the French for "rubbing") was a technique used by Surrealist artist Max Ernst. Place paper over any textured surface and rub a crayon or soft pencil across it — the texture transfers to the paper. Leaves, coins, bricks, wood grain, screens, mesh bags, and fabric all work beautifully. This activity takes 20 minutes and produces beautiful results.

3D / Form

You don't need clay to explore form — rolled newspaper, tape, cardboard tubes, and boxes can all be assembled into three-dimensional sculptures. The key is helping your child think about how something looks from ALL angles, not just the front.

📖The Lesson — Art You Can Almost Touch

Part 1 · Texture — Feeling With Your Eyes

Run your hand along different surfaces right now: a table, a wall, your clothing. Each feels different — smooth, rough, bumpy, soft, hard. That quality — how a surface feels to the touch — is its texture.

In visual art, texture works in two ways:

Actual texture (also called tactile texture) exists in three-dimensional art. When you look at a clay pot, a mosaic, or a relief sculpture, you could reach out and feel the real surface. In painting, when artists pile paint thickly (impasto), the dried paint has actual physical texture — ridges, bumps, and peaks you can feel.

Implied texture (visual texture) exists on flat surfaces — the artist creates the illusion that you could feel a surface, even though the canvas is smooth. An illustrator drawing a wolf uses different brushstrokes for fur than for a snow-covered rock. Your eye reads the difference and your brain "feels" it, even though your fingers can't.

Part 2 · Creating Texture in Art

Artists create texture using many techniques:

  • Hatching — drawing parallel lines close together; closer lines = darker, denser texture
  • Cross-hatching — drawing overlapping lines in two directions, creating a mesh texture
  • Stippling — making tiny dots; more dots = denser, darker
  • Frottage — rubbing paper over a textured surface to transfer the texture
  • Impasto — thick, textured paint applied with a palette knife or brush
  • Collage — attaching materials with different actual textures to the artwork

Part 3 · Form — Art in Three Dimensions

Form is the three-dimensional equivalent of shape. Where a circle is a shape, a sphere is a form. Where a square is a shape, a cube is a form. Form has height, width, AND depth — you can walk around it, look at it from below, feel its weight.

Art that creates form is called sculpture. Sculptors work in many materials: marble, bronze, clay, wood, glass, found objects, fabric, ice, sand, light, and air. Auguste Rodin's "The Thinker," Michelangelo's "David," and Indigenous totem poles are all sculpture.

Part 4 · Form in Everyday Life

Everything in your home has form: the curve of a mug handle, the angles of a chair, the dome of a light fixture. Designers who create objects you use every day (industrial designers) think constantly about form — how an object looks from every angle, how it feels in your hand, how its texture invites or discourages touching.

Part 5 · Famous Textures in Art

Käthe Kollwitz created deeply textured prints and drawings of German working-class families during times of war and poverty — the roughness of the texture reflected the roughness of the lives she depicted. Haida Nation carvers in British Columbia create deeply carved totem poles and masks where the texture of the cedar wood is an essential part of the artwork. In both cases, texture isn't decorative — it's meaningful.

🎨Think Like a Sculptor

Sculptors must think in three dimensions — not just what something looks like from the front, but from all sides, below, and above. They also think about negative space: the empty space carved out of a material, or the spaces between parts of a sculpture, are as designed as the solid parts.

Design challenge: If you were sculpting a monument to celebrate kindness, what form would it take? Not what IMAGE it would show — what SHAPE and FORM? Would it be smooth or textured? Tall or low? Hard-edged or curved? Why?

Touch experiment: Close your eyes and have someone place 5 different objects in your hands one at a time. Without looking, describe each object's texture in at least 3 words. Then draw what you imagined each object looked like, based only on what you felt.

🪨 Braille is a system of raised dots that blind people read by touch. It's a kind of art that communicates entirely through texture. Are there other ways we could use texture as a full language — not just a decorative quality — in art and design?

🪨 Land art (or Earth art) is a movement where artists use the landscape itself as their medium — arranging rocks, carving into cliffs, creating patterns in fields. Robert Smithson's "Spiral Jetty" (a 460-metre spiral made of rocks in the Great Salt Lake in Utah) is one example. This art can only be fully experienced in person, from above, or from certain angles. Is that a limitation or a feature?

🌌 Lesson 5 · Space and Value — Creating Depth

Curriculum: V1.5 · V3.5

✨Wonder Hook — Why Does This Flat Picture Look 3D?

Hold up any photograph. It's completely flat — a thin piece of paper. And yet when you look at it, you see a deep space: trees in the distance look small, close objects look large, and your brain creates a sense of three-dimensional depth from a two-dimensional surface.

Your brain does this automatically, using rules about depth it has learned from a lifetime of looking. Artists figured out these rules and turned them into techniques they could use intentionally. The discovery of formal perspective in the Renaissance changed art, architecture, and mathematics forever.

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Understand positive and negative space and use both intentionally (V1.5)
  • Create the illusion of depth using size, overlap, and placement (V1.5)
  • Use value (light and dark) to suggest form and light source (V3.5)
  • Create a simple drawing that demonstrates at least three depth cues
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

Why this lesson matters

Space and value are what transform flat drawings into convincing images. Once your child understands these concepts, they will literally see art — and the world — differently. They'll notice the depth in photographs, the shadows on faces, the way distant hills fade to pale blue.

Depth cues to teach

There are seven visual cues that create the perception of depth:

  1. Size — closer things appear larger
  2. Overlap — closer objects cover parts of further ones
  3. Placement — things lower on the page appear closer
  4. Detail — closer objects have more detail
  5. Value — closer objects have stronger contrast
  6. Aerial perspective — distant things fade toward blue-grey
  7. Linear perspective — parallel lines converge at a vanishing point

For Grade 1, focus on the first four. Introduce linear perspective as a "magic trick" — you don't need to teach it fully yet.

Value = light and shadow

Value is the scale from pure white to pure black, with every grey in between. Draw a square and shade from white on the left to black on the right — that's a value scale. Show your child that shading one side of a circle darker makes it look like a sphere (a ball).

📖The Lesson — Making Flat Things Look Deep

Part 1 · Positive and Negative Space

Look at any simple silhouette image — a bird against a pale sky, a tree against a sunset. The bird is positive space — the main subject, the "figure." The sky is negative space — the empty background, the "ground."

Clever artists pay as much attention to negative space as to positive space. The shape of the empty space around a subject can be as beautiful — and as meaningful — as the subject itself. The famous FedEx logo has an arrow hidden in the negative space between the E and the X. Japanese woodblock prints often use vast areas of negative space to make a single branch or figure feel monumental.

Part 2 · Creating Depth on a Flat Surface

Your eye learns to read depth cues — visual signals that tell your brain how far away things are. When you learn to USE these cues intentionally in drawing, you can create a convincing three-dimensional image on a flat piece of paper.

Size cue: Draw an apple near the bottom of the page, large. Draw another apple at the top, small. Your brain reads the small apple as being far away — even though both are on the same flat surface. This is because in real life, things further away appear smaller.

Overlap: Draw a circle. Draw another circle partially behind it. The one in front appears closer — even with no other information.

Placement: In most natural scenes, near things sit lower in your field of vision and far things sit higher. Artists use this by placing "close" objects low on the page and "distant" objects high.

Aerial perspective: The further away something is, the more atmosphere is between you and it. This atmosphere makes distant objects look lighter, bluer, and less detailed. When you look at a mountain range, the furthest mountains look pale blue-grey — this is aerial perspective.

Part 3 · Value — Light and Shadow

Value is the scale from the lightest light to the darkest dark. In drawing, value is how you show that a surface is curved, lit, or in shadow.

Think of a sphere (a ball) sitting in front of a lamp. The side facing the lamp is brightest. The side facing away is darkest. The edges gradually shade from light to dark. This gradual shading is called gradation or blending, and it's what makes flat circles look like round balls.

The highlight is the brightest spot — usually a small white area where the light hits directly. The shadow is the darkest area away from the light. The reflected light is a slightly lighter strip at the edge of the shadow, where light bounces back from nearby surfaces.

Part 4 · Linear Perspective — A Magic Trick

In the 1400s, Italian architect Filippo Brunelleschi mathematically discovered the rules of linear perspective: that in a picture, all parallel lines running away from the viewer appear to converge at a single point on the horizon called the vanishing point. Draw a straight road going into the distance and watch the edges converge to a point. This discovery revolutionized Western art and architecture — and it's just geometry.

🎨Think Like an Architect

Architects must constantly think in three dimensions, then represent those 3D spaces on 2D drawings. They use perspective, plan views (looking straight down), elevation views (looking straight at a wall), and section views (a sliced cross-section) to communicate a building from every angle.

Design challenge: Draw a simple room (a bedroom, a classroom, a playroom) using at least three depth cues. Include: a floor, walls, ceiling, at least two pieces of furniture, and a window. Make it look three-dimensional.

Value experiment: Draw 5 circles in a row. Starting with the leftmost, leave it completely white. Shade each subsequent circle a little darker, until the rightmost one is solid black. Now draw a circle and shade it as if a light is coming from the upper left. Can you make it look like a sphere?

🌟Keep Wondering

🌌 Before linear perspective was "discovered" in the European Renaissance, artists in medieval Europe painted people in sizes based on importance — God was largest, kings were next, ordinary people were smallest — regardless of where they stood in the scene. This is called "hierarchical scale." Was that a mistake, or was it a deliberate choice to communicate a different reality (spiritual importance rather than physical distance)?

🖌️ Lesson 6 · Creating Art — Painting and Printmaking

Curriculum: V1.6 · V2.1

✨Wonder Hook — Why Print When You Can Just Paint?

Five hundred years ago, if you wanted to share an image with someone far away, you had only two options: carry the original artwork (a single unique object) to them, or have an artist copy it by hand (which took weeks and was never quite identical).

Then printmaking came along — a way to make multiple identical copies of an image from a single carved or etched surface. The same technology that made the printing press possible also made it possible for artists to share their images across continents. A single printed image could exist in a hundred cities at once.

Wonder: Has the internet done to digital art what printmaking did to visual art 500 years ago?

🎯Learning Objectives

  • Understand the printmaking process: relief, screen, and mono printing (V1.6)
  • Create an original painting and a simple print (V2.1)
  • Describe process and choices made in creating an artwork (V2.1)
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

Two major media today

This lesson covers two creation methods that are new for your child: painting technique (beyond just "put paint on paper") and printmaking (making multiples from a single surface). Both require some materials, but low-budget substitutes work fine.

Painting supplies you need

Watercolour paint (or diluted tempera), brushes in at least two sizes, paper (watercolour paper or cardstock holds up better than copy paper), water, and paper towels. A foam plate or palette for mixing.

Basic painting techniques to demonstrate

  • Wet-on-wet — wet the paper first with clean water; drop colour into it. Colours bloom and blend in unpredictable, beautiful ways. Great for backgrounds and abstract work.
  • Wet-on-dry — dry paper, wet brush with colour. Creates crisp, controlled edges. Good for details.
  • Wash — very diluted paint spread across a large area, creating a thin transparent layer. Sky effects, water.

Printmaking without a press

The simplest print for Grade 1: cut shapes from foam trays (used grocery meat/vegetable trays, washed), roll or brush paint on them, press onto paper. Alternatively: carved potato prints, leaf prints (paint the underside of a leaf and press it), or "monoprinting" (paint a design on a plastic bag and press paper onto it). Any of these work perfectly.

📖The Lesson — Making Marks That Last

Part 1 · Painting — A History as Old as Humanity

The oldest paintings in the world (those Neanderthal cave paintings we mentioned in Lesson 1) were made by mixing pigments (natural colouring agents from minerals and charcoal) with animal fat or water. Modern paints work the same way — a pigment (colour) suspended in a binder (a liquid that makes the colour stick).

Different paints use different binders:

  • Watercolour — pigment + gum arabic; transparent, luminous, hard to correct
  • Tempera — pigment + egg yolk (traditional) or synthetic binder; bright, fast-drying, good for children
  • Acrylic — pigment + acrylic polymer; fast-drying, can be used thickly or thinly, water-soluble when wet
  • Oil paint — pigment + linseed oil; dries slowly (days to months), can be blended easily, was the dominant painting medium from the 1400s to 1900s

Part 2 · Essential Painting Techniques

Wet-on-wet: When you apply wet paint to a wet surface, colours spread and blend in organic, flowing ways. Watercolourists use this for misty backgrounds, skies, and water. The results are unpredictable — which is part of the beauty.

Wet-on-dry: Applying wet paint to a dry surface gives you control. Edges are sharp. You can place a colour exactly where you want it. Most detailed work in watercolour is done wet-on-dry.

Glazing: Painting a transparent layer of colour over a dried layer. The layers interact optically — you see through the top layer to the one below. Old Masters used dozens of thin glazes to build up the glowing skin tones in portraits.

Part 3 · Printmaking — Making Multiples

Printmaking is the process of transferring an image from one surface to another — usually multiple times. The key concept: unlike painting (where you make one unique original), printmaking makes editions — a series of identical (or near-identical) prints.

The main types of printmaking:

  • Relief printing — you carve away the parts you DON'T want to print; what's left (the "relief") gets inked and pressed. Woodblock prints, linocuts, potato prints, and stamp prints all work this way. The image reverses when printed!
  • Intaglio — the opposite of relief; ink goes into engraved grooves rather than on raised surfaces. Copper etchings and engravings are intaglio. Requires metal plates and a press.
  • Screen printing — ink is pushed through a mesh screen; areas blocked with a stencil don't print. T-shirts, posters, and fine art prints use screen printing.
  • Monoprint — a print made from a single painted surface, producing one unique print. Combines painting and printmaking.

Part 4 · Japanese Woodblock Printing

One of the most sophisticated printmaking traditions in history comes from Japan. Artists like Hokusai and Hiroshige created multicolour woodblock prints that required a separate carved block for each colour — printed in perfect registration (alignment) on top of each other. Hokusai's "The Great Wave off Kanagawa" (1831) is one of the most reproduced artworks in history. It was printed from a single carving in large editions that sold to the Japanese middle class — popular art, not museum art, at the time.

🎨Think Like a Printmaker

Printmakers must think in reverse — because the image transfers as a mirror image. Letters and numbers must be carved backwards. This takes spatial thinking that isn't natural for most people.

Experiment: Write your name on a piece of paper. Hold it up to a mirror. That's what a printmaker sees when they carve their printing block. Can you write your name reversed from memory?

Think: Printmaking allows artists to make 50 identical prints of a single image, each of which could be sold separately. Is print number 1 worth more than print number 50? Why? Does the value of art depend on how many copies exist?

🪬 Lesson 7 · Art That Reflects Culture

Curriculum: V2.2 · V3.1 · V3.2

✨Wonder Hook — Who Gets to Tell the Story?

For most of European history, art museums collected and celebrated art from one narrow tradition: primarily Western European art made by men. Indigenous carvings were put in natural history museums (classified as "artefacts," not "art"). Art by women was often attributed to their male relatives. Art from Africa, Asia, and the Americas was called "folk art" or "craft."

Over the last 50 years, this has started to change. Indigenous artists, Black artists, women artists, and artists from around the world are now reclaiming the right to call their work Art — with a capital A.

Your wonder: What is the difference between "art," "craft," "artefact," and "folk art"? Who decides which label something gets?

Why this lesson matters

Children absorb assumptions about whose art "counts" from an early age. This lesson actively disrupts those assumptions by placing art from Indigenous traditions, African traditions, East Asian traditions, and Islamic art alongside European art — not as exotic exceptions, but as equally valid and sophisticated traditions.

Key things to convey

  • Indigenous art (from Canada and around the world) is a living tradition — not something that belonged only to the past
  • Different cultures have different definitions of "art" — some traditions integrate art into everyday objects (a beautifully made basket is also art)
  • All cultural art traditions have their own vocabulary, techniques, and symbolic systems that are worth learning

📖The Lesson — Art as a Mirror of Society

Part 1 · Art Carries Meaning Beyond Beauty

Visual art has never been only about making beautiful things. In every culture and time period, art has also been used to:

  • Record history — Egyptian hieroglyphics combined writing and image; cave paintings recorded animals and hunts
  • Honour the sacred — religious art from every tradition (Christian icons, Hindu temple sculpture, Islamic geometric pattern, Indigenous ceremonial art)
  • Express identity — who we are, where we come from, what we value
  • Question power — political cartoons, protest art, graffiti murals that challenge authority
  • Celebrate community — community murals, quilting bees, public sculpture

Part 2 · Indigenous Art Traditions of Canada

Canada is home to hundreds of distinct Indigenous Nations, each with its own artistic traditions developed over thousands of years.

West Coast First Nations art — characterized by the formline — a thick, flowing outline that creates interconnected positive and negative shapes. Representations of animals (raven, eagle, salmon, orca) carry clan identities and spiritual meaning. Totem poles, masks, bentwood boxes, and woven robes are central art forms.

Inuit art — soapstone carving, bone and ivory carving, and printmaking (Cape Dorset/Kinngait is world-famous for its prints). Subjects include Arctic animals, hunting scenes, transformation figures (half-human, half-animal), and daily life. Artists like Kenojuak Ashevak brought Inuit art to global recognition.

Woodland/Anishinaabe art — Norval Morrisseau developed a style in the 1960s that drew on traditional Anishinaabe pictographs and oral traditions, using bold outlines and bright colours to depict legends and spiritual imagery. He is called the "Picasso of the North."

Plains Nations beadwork — highly refined geometric and floral designs on clothing, moccasins, and regalia, using glass trade beads introduced by European contact. A single pair of elaborately beaded moccasins can take hundreds of hours to create.

Part 3 · Art From Around the World

Islamic geometric art — Islamic tradition has historically avoided realistic depictions of living beings in religious contexts, developing instead an extraordinarily sophisticated tradition of geometric pattern and calligraphy. The intricate tile work of the Alhambra palace in Spain uses mathematical principles that mathematicians only formally proved centuries later.

West African art — masks, bronze casting (the Benin Bronzes, created in what is now Nigeria, were technically equal to anything in Renaissance Europe), textiles (kente cloth from Ghana), and beadwork. Much West African art functions in ceremony and does not exist as a static display piece.

Mexican muralism — in the 1920s, Diego Rivera, Frida Kahlo's husband, led a movement to paint enormous public murals on government buildings, celebrating Indigenous heritage and revolutionary politics. These murals were deliberately public art — for everyone, not just museum visitors.

Part 4 · Art in Canada Today

Canada has one of the most diverse contemporary art scenes in the world. Artists from every background contribute to an ongoing conversation about what it means to be Canadian, what our history includes, and what our future might look like. The National Gallery of Canada, the Art Gallery of Ontario, and hundreds of smaller galleries and Indigenous art centres showcase this work.

🎨Think Like an Art Curator

A curator is someone who selects, arranges, and presents artworks in museums and galleries. The choices a curator makes — which works to include, which to exclude, how to group them, what labels to write — shape how visitors understand art, history, and culture.

💬 Lesson 8 · Reflecting on Art — Critique and Appreciation

Curriculum: V2.1 · V2.2 · V3.1 · V3.2 · V3.3 · V3.4 · V3.5

✨Wonder Hook — What Makes Art Good?

Here is a question that has troubled philosophers, critics, and artists for centuries: Is there such a thing as objective quality in art? Is the Mona Lisa objectively better than a child's drawing? Or is beauty truly in the eye of the beholder?

Some people argue that technical skill, historical influence, and emotional depth can be measured and compared objectively. Others argue that all aesthetic judgement is subjective — what moves you, moves you, and no one can tell you you're wrong about what you feel.

Your wonder: Is your opinion of an artwork valid even if you know nothing about art history or technique? Does knowledge change what you feel?

🎯Learning Objectives
  • Apply the four-step critique method (describe, analyse, interpret, evaluate) to artworks (V2.1, V2.2)
  • Use all seven elements of art as analytical tools (V3.1–V3.5)
  • Distinguish between describing what you see and interpreting what it means
  • Write a formal, evidence-based art critique
👩‍👧Parent Teaching Guide

The goal of this lesson

Art critique is a formal skill — it's how artists, students, curators, and critics communicate about artworks. The four-step model (Describe → Analyse → Interpret → Evaluate) gives your child a reliable framework they can apply to any artwork for the rest of their life. This isn't about "being right" — it's about thinking with precision and using evidence.

The most important distinction to teach

Describe = what you actually see (facts, not opinions)
Interpret = what you think it means (opinions, supported by description)

Many beginners jump straight to interpretation ("It looks sad") without first describing what specific visual evidence makes them say that. The discipline of describing first is the key skill to build.

How to do this together

Pick one artwork and go through the four steps out loud together. You model the process first: "I can see a woman in a blue dress sitting by a window. The light comes from the left. There are dark shadows on the right side of her face." Then ask your child to add more descriptions. Only AFTER thorough description do you both move to interpretation.

How to respond to your child's critique

The only question you should ask is: "What in the artwork makes you say that?" This pushes them back to visual evidence every time. Never say an interpretation is wrong — but ask for its evidence.

📖The Lesson — How to Talk About Art

Part 1 · Why Art Critique Matters

You don't need to critique art to enjoy it. But the ability to think and speak clearly about art teaches you something valuable: the skill of looking deeply at anything — a scientific problem, a piece of writing, a news story, a person's behaviour — and describing it carefully before you judge it.

Art criticism is a way of practising clear thinking.

Part 2 · The Four Steps of Art Critique

Art educators use a four-step framework that works with any artwork:

Step 1 — Describe: What do you literally see? List every visible element without interpretation. What objects are present? What colours? What shapes and lines? How large is it? What medium is it? No opinions allowed in this step — only observable facts.

Example: "I see a woman sitting at a table. She is wearing dark clothing. There is a window behind her. The colours are mostly brown and grey. The paint is applied in thin, smooth layers."

Step 2 — Analyse: How did the artist use the elements of art (line, shape, form, colour, value, texture, space)? What principles of design are present — balance, rhythm, emphasis, contrast, unity? This step looks at HOW the artwork is made, not what it means.

Example: "The light comes from the upper left, creating strong contrast between light and shadow on the figure. The composition uses a triangle formed by the figure's head, elbow, and the corner of the table."

Step 3 — Interpret: What do you think the artwork means or is trying to communicate? What mood does it create? What story is being told? You MUST support your interpretation with evidence from your description and analysis.

Example: "I think the woman feels lonely, because she is alone, the colours are cold and grey, and she is looking down, not at the viewer."

Step 4 — Evaluate: How successful do you think the artwork is? Did the artist achieve what they were trying to do? This is your judgment — based on everything you've described, analysed, and interpreted. Support your evaluation with evidence from the previous steps.

Part 3 · Talking About Your Own Work

The same four steps work when you talk about your own art. Artists often write artist's statements — short texts that describe their process and intention. An artist's statement isn't a self-critique; it's an explanation of choices. Why did you use those colours? What were you trying to express? What would you do differently?

Part 4 · Learning From Great Critics

Art critics use many of the same skills as writers — precise description, clear argument, evidence-based judgment, and original perspective. The best art criticism helps readers see something in an artwork they would have missed. John Berger's book and TV series "Ways of Seeing" (1972) changed how many people understand art's relationship to power and society. You don't need to agree with a critic to learn from them.

Part 5 · Your Growing Art Eye

You've spent this strand developing an eye for art — learning the seven elements, exploring colour, texture, form, space, painting, printmaking, and cultural context. The best way to develop your art eye further is to look at art regularly, draw and create regularly, and talk about what you see. Every time you look at an artwork, you're building a vocabulary of visual experience that will last your entire life.

🎨Think Like an Art Critic

Art critics must write clearly about something that is often felt rather than thought. They must translate visual experience into precise language. This requires both deep looking and strong writing.

Full critique: Choose any artwork (search online, or use one from an earlier lesson). Apply all four steps in writing. Be thorough: your description should be at least 5–7 sentences; your analysis 4–5 sentences; your interpretation 3–4 sentences; your evaluation 3–4 sentences.

Meta-question: After completing the critique, did you feel differently about the artwork than you did before you started? Why might careful looking change how we feel about something?

🌟Keep Wondering

💬 Some scientists study how looking at art affects the brain. They've found that when people look at a painting they find beautiful, the same area of the brain that activates when they look at someone they love is activated. Does that mean beauty and love are neurologically related? What would that mean?

💬 AI systems can now create images that many people find beautiful and moving, indistinguishable from human-made art. But AI doesn't experience emotion, doesn't have intention, doesn't have a life history. If the output looks the same, does the process matter? Is the making of art as important as the art itself?

💬 You have spent a whole school year studying art. Has your everyday life changed? Do you see the world differently? Do you notice lines, shapes, colours, and textures in places you ignored before? What would change about your life if you stopped paying attention to visual art?

📖 Vocabulary — All Key Words

Every important word from this strand. Study these throughout the year!

elements of art
The seven building blocks every visual artist uses: line, shape, form, colour, value, texture, and space
"She pointed to the painting and named all seven elements of art."
composition
The arrangement of elements within an artwork
"The photographer thought carefully about the composition before taking the shot."
contour drawing
A drawing that traces the outline and interior edges of a subject
"In blind contour drawing, you look at your subject, not your paper."
impasto
A painting technique where paint is applied thickly so the surface has texture
"Van Gogh used impasto so that the paint itself had expressive texture."
value
The lightness or darkness of a colour or surface
"She used shading to create value and make the apple look three-dimensional."
relief printing
A printmaking method where ink is applied to a raised surface and pressed onto paper
"They carved a design into a foam tray for relief printing."
negative space
The empty area around and between subjects in a composition
"The artist paid as much attention to the negative space as to the subject itself."
depth cues
Visual signals that help a viewer perceive distance in a flat image
"Overlap and size difference are two important depth cues in landscape drawing."
perspective
A system for representing three-dimensional space on a two-dimensional surface
"Linear perspective uses a vanishing point to show depth."
primary colours
Red, yellow, and blue — the colours that cannot be made by mixing other colours
"All other colours on the colour wheel come from the three primary colours."
complementary colours
Colours that sit opposite each other on the colour wheel and intensify each other
"Orange and blue are complementary colours; together they vibrate with energy."
critique
A structured analysis of an artwork using the steps: describe, analyse, interpret, evaluate
"After the studio session, the students shared critiques of each other's paintings."
organic shapes
Irregular, flowing shapes found in nature
"Leaves, clouds, and puddles are all examples of organic shapes."
frottage
A technique of making texture rubbings by placing paper over a surface and rubbing a pencil or crayon over it
"Max Ernst used frottage to discover unexpected textures for his Surrealist paintings."
warm colours
Reds, oranges, and yellows — colours associated with warmth, energy, and fire
"The sunset painting used only warm colours to create a feeling of heat."
hue
The pure name of a colour on the colour spectrum
"Crimson and scarlet are different versions of the same hue: red."
